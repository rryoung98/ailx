# Q4 — What does the assessment / psychometrics literature say about scoring PROCESS?

## VERDICT

**The hypothesis "collect but do not score" is REFUTED as a universal rule, and REPLACED by a sharper
and worse one for AILX.**

Three findings, in order of force:

1. **"Collect but do not score" is the rule for the *population-monitoring* programmes (NAEP, PIAAC,
   and PISA outside the 2012 problem-solving domain).** NAEP's own technical documentation says score
   scales are estimated by IRT from *item responses*; the process-data logs are a separate research
   release for secondary analysis. Confirmed from two NCES pages.

2. **But it is NOT the rule everywhere. Process IS scored operationally in at least two places, and in
   BOTH of them the scoring rule is EXPLICITLY NON-MONOTONE IN VOLUME — extra actions LOSE you points.**
   - PISA 2012 problem solving: MP3 PLAYER Item 2 gives **full credit only for ≤ 13 clicks**; more
     clicks to the same goal state gets **partial credit**. Log data was also used to **remove** credit
     where the sequence showed guessing.
   - USMLE Step 3 Computer-based Case Simulations: the transaction list *is* the score. Official USMLE
     text: *"If you order something that is unnecessary and excessive, your score will decrease"* and
     *"You will be scored lower if you take an aggressive approach when restraint and observation are
     the standard of care."*

   **This is the exact inverse of AILX `processSignal`.** The only two high-stakes precedents for
   scoring process both penalise the behaviour AILX rewards. AILX's rule
   `0.5*min(1, distinctPrompts/N) + 0.5*min(1, cycles/M)` is, in the language of these programmes, a
   rule that gives full credit for *inefficiency*.

3. **Where process indicators have been validated, the validated quantity is a STRATEGY PATTERN or an
   ADAPTIVE ALLOCATION, never a raw count.** PISA 2012 CPS validation used VOTAT (vary-one-thing-at-a-time),
   a specific structure in the action sequence. Naumann (2019, PISA 2009 log files, N = 32,669, open
   access, fetched) shows the sign of the time-on-task effect **flips**: comprehension skill predicts
   *more* time on hard tasks (b = +0.26) and *less* time on easy tasks (b = −0.08). A monotone volume
   indicator is therefore not merely weak; it is measuring different things in different directions
   depending on the task, and it is partly driven by *reading enjoyment* — a motivational,
   construct-irrelevant variable.

**No source found that supports a volume-monotone process score.** Searched OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar,
Crossref, Exa, arXiv-adjacent. This is a genuine negative result: the claim "more prompts and more
revision cycles = more competence" has, as far as this search reaches, **no published psychometric
support in any operational assessment programme.** The nearest published analogue — essay length in
automated essay scoring — is the field's standard cautionary tale, not a precedent.

### 1. Goldhammer, Hahnel, Kroehne & Zehner (2021) — the framework paper. FETCHED IN FULL.
*"From byproduct to design factor: on validating the interpretation of process indicators based on log
data."* **Large-scale Assessments in Education 9, 20.**
https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40536-021-00113-5 (open access)

**Classification: CONTRADICTS.**

Exact findings, quoted:

- On what a raw count/time indicator means: *"The way they are defined and constructed determines the
  degree of interpretative ambiguity... For instance, a time on task indicator does not include much
  information; it is not self-explanatory, but needs to be interpreted based on additional information
  such as item and person characteristics."* AILX's `distinctPrompts` and `cycles` are exactly this
  class of indicator — the lowest-information, highest-ambiguity class the paper names.
- The named failure mode is **construct-irrelevant variance**: *"other sources of variance apart from
  the target construct affect the observed behavioral differences captured in the indicator. That is,
  reasoning from the observations to the target construct becomes ambiguous."*
- The paper's central prescription is the title: process indicators must move **from byproduct to
  design factor**. You must design the task so that the action pattern is diagnostic, then validate the
  interpretation empirically (nomothetic span or construct representation). AILX has done neither.
- Crucially, the paper **does not say process must not be scored**. It says the *scoring inference*
  ("evidence identification rules appropriately extract the targeted behavioral features") must be
  justified before the *explanation inference* is available. It explicitly cites PISA 2012 as a case
  where process entered scoring: *"if efficiency is part of the competence construct, full credit is
  given for a correct work product only if the number of actions falls below a certain threshold;
  otherwise, partial credit is given (e.g., problem solving in PISA 2012)."*
- This is the source that pointed to the PISA 2012 fact in §3 below. Note the direction: **below** a
  threshold, not above.

### 2. OECD (2014) — PISA 2012 Results Volume V. FETCHED IN FULL (PDF → text).
*"PISA 2012 Results: Creative Problem Solving — Students' Skills in Tackling Real-Life Problems
(Volume V)."* OECD Publishing. ISBN 978-92-64-20807-0.
https://www.oecd.org/content/dam/oecd/en/publications/reports/2014/04/pisa-2012-results-creative-problem-solving-volume-v_g1g3b025/9789264208070-en.pdf

**Classification: CONTRADICTS (hard). This is the single most damaging source for AILX's design.**

Exact text, verbatim from the fetched document:

> "One of the innovative features of the problem-solving assessment is that information contained in
> log files about the sequence of actions performed by students was used to inform scoring of items
> where appropriate. For example, when it could be established that students had guessed an answer,
> they received no credit for that answer."

> [MP3 PLAYER Item 2] "...process information captured by the computer (in this case, how many steps
> the student takes to successfully reach the goal state) contributes to the score. The task is to be
> completed using as few clicks as possible... If the number of clicks used (no more than 13) indicates
> that students have been efficient in reaching the goal they receive full credit; but if they reach
> the goal in a less-efficient manner (more than 13 clicks), they only receive partial credit."
> Field-trial result: **~39% full credit, ~33% partial credit.**

> [TICKETS Item 2] "If students visit both screens before buying the cheapest ticket... they are given
> full credit. Students who buy one of the two tickets without comparing the prices for the two only
> earn partial credit."

Read these three together and the PISA design principle is explicit:
- **Volume is penalised** (>13 clicks ⇒ downgrade).
- **A specific, theory-named action pattern is required** (visit both screens = targeted exploration;
  the requirement is a *particular* comparison, not "explore more").
- **Process data is used to SUBTRACT credit** from work products that look right but were reached by an
  unjustified route.

PISA 2012 is a real, published, operational, cross-national precedent for scoring process. It scores it
in the direction opposite to AILX on both terms.

### 3. USMLE / NBME — Computer-based Case Simulations, official scoring description. FETCHED IN FULL.
https://www.usmle.org/exam-resources/step-3-materials/step-3-test-question-formats/computer-based-case-simulations
(In operational use in USMLE Step 3 since November 1999; still current — verified the page is live.)

**Classification: CONTRADICTS.**

This is the highest-stakes assessment found in which **the process IS the scored object** — the
examinee's ordered transaction list in a patient simulation, not any final answer. Verbatim from the
official page:

> "The timing and sequencing of indicated actions, as well as the commission of actions that are not
> indicated or are potentially harmful, are aggregated in your evaluation. **Indicated patient
> management actions are awarded credit while actions that are not indicated and pose greater potential
> risk to a patient decrease your score.** Seemingly correct management decisions made in an incorrect
> sequence or after a delay in simulated time may receive no credit."

> "In some cases there may be very little for you to do to manage a patient. In those instances, you
> will be scored on your ability to recognize situations in which the most appropriate action is to
> **refrain from, or defer,** testing and treatment. **You will be scored lower if you take an
> aggressive approach when restraint and observation are the standard of care.** The best overall
> strategy is to balance efficiency with thoroughness."

> "If you order something that is unnecessary and excessive, your score will decrease."

> "The scoring process uses algorithms that represent **codified expert physician-defined criteria**...
> obtained from expert physicians... For each case, the input of expert generalists and specialists is
> obtained."

The three structural facts that make this work, and that AILX lacks:
1. Scoring weights are **per-case and expert-derived**, not a single global formula.
2. Many actions carry **zero** weight ("counseling, diet, ambulation... tend to carry little or no
   weight in scoring unless they are particularly relevant to the case"). AILX gives every deduped
   prompt equal positive weight.
3. There is a **negative region**. The score is non-monotone in action count by construction.

The AILX analogue would be: a prompt that made the artefact worse must cost points, and a prompt that
was irrelevant must cost nothing. `processSignal` has no negative region and no zero region.

### 7. Naumann (2019) — time on task is NOT monotone. FETCHED IN FULL, open access.
*"The Skilled, the Knowledgeable, and the Motivated: Investigating the Strategic Allocation of Time on
Task in a Computer-Based Assessment."* **Frontiers in Psychology 10, 1429.**
https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.01429/full

**Classification: CONTRADICTS.**

Design: PISA 2009 computer-based digital-reading log files. **N = 32,669** students, **19 countries**
(country-wise 930 ≤ N ≤ 2,800), mean age 15.78. Linear mixed models per country, meta-analytically
integrated. Time on task read directly from log files.

Exact numbers, quoted from the fetched Results:

> "In hard tasks, there was a strong positive association of comprehension skill with both total time on
> task, **b = 0.26 (SE = 0.01), 95%-CI: [0.23; 0.28]**, and time on relevant pages, b = 0.23 (SE = 0.01),
> 95%-CI: [0.22; 0.26]. In easy tasks, in contrast, this association was **negative** for both total time
> on task, **b = −0.08 (SE = 0.01), 95%-CI: [−0.10; −0.07]**, and time on relevant pages, b = −0.06
> (SE = 0.01), 95%-CI: [−0.07; −0.05]."

And for strategy knowledge: b = +0.07 on hard tasks, reversing to **b = −0.02** on easy tasks.

Two consequences for AILX, both direct:

- **The sign of a volume indicator's relation to skill depends on the task.** A single monotone formula
  applied across a mixed T1 build cannot be right for all of it. Skilled candidates spend *less* effort
  where effort is not required. `processSignal` marks them down for it.
- **Volume is driven by construct-irrelevant dispositions.** The paper's headline is that *reading
  enjoyment* — a motivational variable, not a skill — significantly predicts time-on-task allocation
  alongside comprehension skill. Translating: candidate enthusiasm for chatting with an AI would load on
  `distinctPrompts` regardless of AI-literacy. That is the "vibecoder" failure mode from the hiring
  reports, reproduced in a peer-reviewed PISA analysis with n = 32,669.

### 8. Greiff, Wüstenberg & Avvisati (2015) — what a VALIDATED process indicator looks like. ABSTRACT ONLY.
*"Computer-generated log-file analyses as a window into students' minds? A showcase study based on the
PISA 2012 assessment of problem solving."* **Computers & Education 91, 92–105.**
DOI 10.1016/j.compedu.2015.10.018 (173 citations)

**Classification: QUALIFIES.** UNVERIFIED (abstract only) — ScienceDirect and two mirrors served
CAPTCHA/bot walls; no full text obtained. Findings below are the authors' own abstract wording.

*"We analyzed log-file data from the CPS item Climate Control encompassing N = 16,219 students from 44
countries and economies... Results indicated that (1) the VOTAT strategy [vary-one-thing-at-a-time] was
strongly related to performance on the Climate Control item as well as to overall problem solving
proficiency, and (2) that there were different levels of non-mastery that ranged from applying no
systematic strategic behavior to actually applying VOTAT but still failing to solve the item."*

The construct that predicted proficiency was **VOTAT — a named, theory-derived structural property of
the action sequence** (each intervention isolates one variable). It is not "number of interventions".
The paper also reports a group who *did* apply VOTAT and still failed, i.e. the process indicator and
the outcome indicator dissociate. No effect sizes are quoted here because I did not read the body.

The AILX-shaped question this raises: is there any structural property of a prompt sequence that plays
the VOTAT role? `distinctPrompts` is not it. Something like "the candidate's later prompts reference and
correct a specific defect present in the earlier output" might be.

### 9. Koretz, Stecher, Klein & McCaffrey (1994) — a documented failure in a public-reporting programme. FETCHED IN FULL.
*"The Vermont Portfolio Assessment Program: Findings and Implications."* **Educational Measurement:
Issues and Practice 13(3), 5–16.** DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3992.1994.tb00443.x (205 citations; DOI verified
via Crossref). Full RAND/ERIC report text fetched: ERIC ED365699,
https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED365699.pdf

**Classification: QUALIFIES — the closest thing found to a documented failure of scoring complex,
loosely-standardised work in a consequential programme.** It is a portfolio (product) programme, not a
log-data programme, so it is an analogue rather than a direct hit. Say so if it is cited.

Exact numbers, quoted from the fetched report:

> "The rater reliability of portfolio scores in both mathematics and writing was **very low**... For
> individual pieces on a single dimension, the correlations of scores between raters ranged from **.28 to
> .57**; the average correlations (across all dimensions) ranged from **.34 to .43**... The percentage of
> cases in which raters agreed on a score was generally **not much higher than expected by chance**."

> "Summing across all pieces within a portfolio on a single scoring dimension raised the average
> reliability coefficients only slightly, to a range of **.38 to .49**... even summing across dimensions
> as well as pieces to get a single total score would have raised the average reliability coefficients
> only to a range of **.49 to .60**."

One worked example from the report: raters agreed on 45% of portfolios where **chance alone gives 35%**
— **kappa = 0.17**, Spearman rho = 0.35.

The consequence, and this is the part that matters:

> "In 1991-92, many of the aggregate statistics the state wished to report were not of high enough
> quality to use, and the **State Department of Education accordingly declined to release them**."

> "One effect of unreliable scoring is to spread out the distribution of scores, so that too many
> students receive high and low scores and too few receive scores near the middle of the distribution."

Named causes: *"inadequate scoring rubrics, insufficient training, and the lack of standardization of
tasks. Unstandardized tasks are likely to complicate scoring substantially."*

The transferable lesson: a *plausible* rubric over *non-standardised* complex work produced a score
that a state government eventually refused to publish. The failure was not detected by design review;
it was detected by measuring inter-rater agreement against chance. AILX has no equivalent measurement
of `processSignal` against anything.

### 12. Ercikan, Guo & He (2020) — the fairness argument. ABSTRACT ONLY.
*"Use of Response Process Data to Inform Group Comparisons and Fairness Research."* **Educational
Assessment 25(3).** DOI 10.1080/10627197.2020.1804353 (44 citations). Authors at ETS.

**Classification: QUALIFIES.** UNVERIFIED (abstract only) — Taylor & Francis served a navigation shell,
no article body.

Abstract: *"We propose two statistical approaches for identifying differential response processes which
extend the differential item functioning (DIF) detection methods... Our findings demonstrate the use of

The implication for AILX is worth stating even from the abstract, because it is structural rather than

### 13. Wise, Bhola & Yang (2006) — the standard operational use of process data. ABSTRACT ONLY.
*"Taking the Time to Improve the Validity of Low-Stakes Tests: The Effort-Monitoring CBT."* **Educational
Measurement: Issues and Practice 25(2).** DOI 10.1111/j.1745-3992.2006.00054.x

**Classification: SUPPORTS "collect but do not score".** UNVERIFIED (abstract only).

Response-time process data is used to detect **rapid-guessing** and either warn the examinee mid-test or
filter the response from analysis: *"the computer monitors examinee effort (based on item response time)
in a low-stakes test and displays warning messages to those exhibiting rapid-guessing behavior... an
effort-monitoring CBT increased examinee effort and yielded **more valid test scores** than a conventional
CBT."*

This is the dominant operational pattern across NAEP, PIAAC and PISA, and it is worth naming plainly for
the AILX write-up: **process data is used to invalidate responses, not to award points.** It is a
*filter*, in the negative direction. Compare PISA 2012's use of logs to withdraw credit for guessed
answers (§2). If AILX wants the safest defensible use of `distinctPrompts` and `cycles`, this is it:
detect a candidate who did not engage with the AI at all, and flag or invalidate the T1 sitting — rather
than award 25 of 160 points on a sliding scale.

## WHAT WAS SEARCHED AND NOT FOUND (negative results, stated plainly)

- **No published study was found that validates a volume-monotone process indicator against any external
  criterion of work quality.** Searched OpenAlex, Semantic Scholar, Crossref and Exa on combinations of
  {process data, log data, action count, number of actions, scoring, validity, high stakes}. The count-
  based indicators that appear in the literature appear as *inputs to be validated* (Goldhammer 2021) or
  as *predictors in exploratory ML feature sets*, not as endorsed scoring rules.
- **No documented case was found of a testing programme that scored process by volume and then had to
  withdraw it.** This is an absence of evidence, not evidence of absence — it plausibly reflects that no
  programme tried. The nearest documented withdrawal is Vermont (§9), which is about rubric-scored
  portfolios, and the nearest documented gaming is essay length in AES (§10), which is about a product
  feature. Neither is a clean precedent. **Do not overclaim either one.**
- **Bergner & von Davier (2019), Greiff et al. (2015), Perelman (2014), Clauser et al. (1997), Ercikan
  et al. (2020) and Wise et al. (2006) were NOT read in full.** Paywalls and bot walls. Every claim
  attributed to them above is either an abstract quotation or a quotation from a fetched opposing reply,
  and is labelled as such at the top of each entry.
- **PISA 2015+ log-file position was not pinned down.** The PISA 2015 Technical Report and the PISA 2015
  Database page were located (both OECD, URLs in the search record) but I did not extract a statement
  about whether 2015+ log data enters scoring in any domain. PISA 2012 problem solving (§2) is the
  verified case. Treat 2015+ as an open question rather than assuming the 2012 practice continued.

## THE ONE-PARAGRAPH ANSWER FOR THE TEN-80 WRITE-UP

Process data is scored in operational high-stakes assessment — PISA 2012 problem solving and USMLE Step 3
CCS both do it — so "collect but do not score" is too strong as a general claim, though it is accurate for
NAEP and PIAAC, whose scale scores are estimated from item responses alone. But every operational
process-scoring rule that was found shares three properties that AILX's `processSignal` lacks: the weights
are per-task and derived from expert judgement of *that* task, a large fraction of possible actions carry
zero weight, and unnecessary or excessive action **reduces** the score. PISA gives full credit for
reaching the goal in ≤ 13 clicks and downgrades to partial credit above that; USMLE states outright that
"if you order something that is unnecessary and excessive, your score will decrease". AILX rewards exactly
what these two programmes penalise. The supporting evidence points the same way: the only process
indicators with published validity evidence are structural strategy patterns such as VOTAT, and the
best-measured volume indicator — time on task, in PISA 2009 log files with N = 32,669 — changes sign
between hard and easy tasks (b = +0.26 vs. b = −0.08 with comprehension skill) and is partly driven by
enjoyment rather than skill. There is no published support for a monotone-in-volume process score, and
the field's own cautionary tale about a monotone-in-volume feature is essay length in automated essay
scoring.
