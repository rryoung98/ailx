# Q5 — Counter-evidence against a volume-monotone `processSignal`

**Scope.** AILX T1 awards 25/160 points as
`0.5*min(1, distinctPrompts/N) + 0.5*min(1, cycles/M)`.
Both terms are monotone non-decreasing in candidate action volume, capped. Nothing checks whether a
prompt improved the artefact. This file collects published counter-evidence. Sources were fetched
unless marked otherwise.

---

## VERDICT

**The formula as written is indefensible as a scored construct measure. Three independent literatures
converge against it, and the strongest objection is not "gaming" — it is that the formula's core
premise (more process = more skill) is empirically false in the one large-scale study that tested it
directly.**

1. **Monotonicity is contradicted, not merely unproven.** Goldhammer et al. (2014, *JEP*, N = 1,020,
   PIAAC German field test) found the time-on-task effect *reverses sign* by task type: positive in
   problem solving, **negative in reading**, and in reading "the more negative, the easier a task
   was" and **more negative as skill increased**. A process-volume term has no uniform sign. Our
   formula hard-codes one sign.
2. **It is trivially gameable, and gaming is anti-correlated with learning.** Baker et al. (2004,
   CHI) defined "gaming the system" as *rapid, systematic, high-volume* interaction with a tutor's
   help and feedback. Gaming frequency correlated **r = −0.38** with post-test (partial r = −0.34
   after controlling prior knowledge and general achievement) — a relationship as strong as prior
   domain knowledge itself (r = 0.32). Our metric would score exactly those students highest.
3. **Even performance assessments inflate under stakes.** Koretz & Barron (1998, RAND MR-1014) —
   a *performance* assessment (KIRIS), chosen precisely to resist multiple-choice-style coaching,
   still inflated: 4th-grade reading gains had **no echo in NAEP at all**; math NAEP gains were
   ~**1/4** the size of KIRIS gains; science ACT gains ~**1/5**. Campbell's Law does not spare
   process/performance formats.
4. **It is unstable across tasks.** Shavelson, Baxter & Gao (1993): person×task interaction was the
   dominant error source in every dataset — **82% / 49% / 48%** of total variance (Science / Math /
   CAP), **60% / 55%** for Navy / Marine job performance. One T1 task cannot generalize.
5. **It has plausible language and familiarity bias, but the direct evidence is missing.** I found
   *no published study* measuring whether non-native English speakers issue more or longer prompts to
   an AI assistant. That is a real, reportable gap. The adjacent evidence (below) is suggestive only.

**If the team keeps a process signal, it must not be volume-monotone and it should not be worth 25
points on one task.** The safest defensible use of these logs is as a *flag* (e.g. detecting
non-engagement, as Wise & Kong do), not as a scored, weighted, monotone component.

**Exactly how our formula gets gamed** (mechanical, no cleverness required):

| Attack | Cost to attacker | Effect on score |
|---|---|---|
| Split one good prompt into N trivially-different prompts ("do X"; "do X, carefully"; "do X please") | seconds | `distinctPrompts` term saturates. Dedup on exact/near-exact text does not stop paraphrase. |
| Ask N throwaway questions ("what's your name?", "summarise the brief") never used in the artefact | seconds | Full 12.5 points. Nothing links a prompt to the artefact. |
| Make M cosmetic edits (whitespace, rename a variable, revert) between prompts to manufacture cycles | seconds | Full 12.5 points. Nothing checks the revision improved anything. |
| Spawn parallel subagents / brute-force high token spend (the exact HN behaviour) | none | Maximises both terms simultaneously. |
| Conversely: one expert prompt that solves the task first time | expert skill | **Scores near zero on 25 points.** |

The last row is the inversion the hiring managers described. Our formula reproduces it by design.

## A. GAMEABILITY / GOODHART

### A1. Baker, Corbett, Koedinger & Wagner (2004) — CONTRADICTS. *Full paper fetched.*
"Off-Task Behavior in the Cognitive Tutor Classroom: When Students 'Game the System'", *CHI 2004*,
pp. 383–390. PDF: https://learninganalytics.upenn.edu/ryanbaker/p383-baker-rev.pdf

Definition, quoted: gaming is "behavior aimed at obtaining correct answers and advancing within the
tutoring curriculum by systematically taking advantage of regularities in the software's feedback and
help" — coded operationally as "**systematic and rapid** incorrect answers or use of help, with
**several such actions taking place during the 20-second observation period**". Examples: "quickly
and repeatedly asking for help until the tutor gives the student the correct answer"; "entering
1,2,3,4… or clicking every checkbox … until the tutor identifies a correct answer".

Exact numbers (n ≈ 70 students, 5 classes, quantitative field observation):
- Gaming frequency vs post-test: F(1,68) = 11.82, p < 0.01, **r = −0.38**. Only off-task category
  significantly related to learning.
- Controlling pre-test and general academic achievement: F(1,59) = 7.73, p < 0.01,
  **partial r = −0.34**.
- For comparison: prior knowledge → post-test r = 0.32; general academic achievement → post-test
  r = 0.36. So gaming is as predictive as prior knowledge, in the opposite direction.
- Off-task talking r = −0.19 (n.s., p = 0.12); inactivity r = −0.08 (n.s.); off-task solitary
  r = −0.08 (n.s.). All *combined* off-task r = −0.33.
- Gaming vs pre-test: r = −0.27, p = 0.02. "all of the high-gaming students had low pre-test scores".
- Observer agreement: r = 0.99 on overall proportions, r = 0.79 per student.

**Why this is fatal for us.** Gaming is *defined by high action volume in a short window*. A metric
that counts prompts and revision cycles is a gaming-frequency counter with the sign flipped. In this
study the students who produced the most actions were the least skilled and learned the least.

### A2. Koretz & Barron (1998) — CONTRADICTS. *Summary page fetched; full monograph not fetched.*
"The Validity of Gains in Scores on the Kentucky Instructional Results Information System (KIRIS)",
RAND MR-1014-EDU. https://www.rand.org/pubs/monograph_reports/MR1014.html

Quoted findings: KIRIS was "a complex, partially performance-based assessment … an archetype of this
wave of reform", adopted specifically "to avoid the degradation of instruction and inflation of test
scores that often occurred when educators were held accountable for scores on multiple-choice tests".
It inflated anyway:
- "large KIRIS gains in fourth-grade reading from 1992 to 1994 **had no echo in NAEP scores**";
- 4th/8th-grade math: "Kentucky's NAEP gains were **roughly one-fourth as large as the KIRIS gains**";
- high-school math/reading KIRIS gains "were **not reflected** in their scores on the ACT";
- "KIRIS science gains were accompanied by ACT gains **only one-fifth as large**";
- internal warning sign: schools with large gains showed "larger than average discrepancies in
  performance between new and reused test items, suggesting that teachers had coached students
  narrowly".
Conclusion, quoted: "inflation of scores remains a risk in assessment-based accountability systems
**even when they rely on test formats other than multiple choice**."

**UNVERIFIED (full text):** I read the RAND abstract page, not MR-1014 itself. The numbers above are
the publisher's own summary numbers, not my reading of the tables.

### A3. Koretz (2008), "Test-Based Educational Accountability" — supporting context.
**UNVERIFIED (search-tool text extract only; PDF not fetched.)**
https://doi.org/10.25656/01:4376 quotes Campbell (1975): "The more any quantitative social indicator
is used for social decision making, the more subject it will be to corruption pressures and the more
apt it will be to distort and corrupt the social processes it is intended to monitor." Cited here as
the framing statement, not as evidence.

### A4. Gao, Schulman & Hilton (2022) — CONTRADICTS (mechanism). *Full PDF fetched.*
"Scaling Laws for Reward Model Overoptimization", arXiv:2210.10760.
https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.10760

Empirically fitted functional forms for gold-reward score `R` as a function of
`d := sqrt(D_KL(π‖π_init))`:
- best-of-n: `R_bon(d) = d(α_bon − β_bon·d)`
- RL: `R_RL(d) = d(α_RL − β_RL·log d)`

Both are **inverted-U in d**: proxy optimization first raises and then *lowers* true performance.
Coefficients scale smoothly with proxy reward-model size. Relevance: this is the quantitative modern
statement of Goodhart for an optimised proxy. Candidates optimising `processSignal` are exactly a
policy optimising a proxy. Note the honest limit — this is a synthetic RLHF setup with a gold reward
model, *not* an assessment study. It supplies the mechanism, not human evidence.

## B. INSTABILITY ACROSS TASKS

### B1. Shavelson, Baxter & Gao (1993) — CONTRADICTS. *Full text fetched (30 pp).*
"Sampling Variability of Performance Assessments", CRESST Final Deliverable ED359229 (also *Journal
of Educational Measurement* 30(3), 215–232). PDF: http://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED359229.pdf

G-study variance components, quoted exactly:

**p × r × t × o design** (science; n_p = 26, 2 raters, 2 tasks, 2 occasions):
- person × task × occasion = **59%** of total variability (largest error source)
- person × task = **32%**
- rater, p×r, r×t, r×o, t×o, p×r×t, r×t×o components ≈ 0 (several set to zero from negative estimates)
- Generalizability coefficients with one task, one rater, one occasion: **ρ² = .04 and Φ = .04.**

**Five p × r × t G-studies:** person × task interaction was "consistently the major source of
measurement error accounting for **82%, 49%, and 48%** of the total variability for the Science, Math
and CAP data"; Navy and Marine Corps job performance: **60%** and **55%**.

**p × t × m design** (n_p = 186, 2 tasks, 4 methods): person 0.84 = 19%; task 0.70 = 16%;
method 0.70 = 16%; **p×t 0.73 = 16%**; p×m 0.14 = 3%; t×m 0.12 = 3%; residual p×t×m,e 1.30 = **29%**.
Method main effect is as large as the person effect — students averaged 3.90 on computer simulation,
3.35 observed, 3.21 notebook, **1.85 short-answer**.

**D-study:** to reach G ≈ .80 for a school mean (absolute decision) needed "about 50 students within
a school … tested on **15 tasks**; or about 100 students on 12 tasks."

Authors' conclusion, quoted: "task sampling variability appears to be **fact, not artifact**", and
"even parallel tasks (e.g., the six mystery boxes) show considerable person × task sampling
variability", and "**Methods do not converge.**"

**Direct implication for AILX.** T1 is one task, one method. If a *scored artefact* on a single
performance task carries 48–82% person×task error, a *behavioural byproduct* of that same single task
carries at least that much. 25 points of 160 on a single-task process count is not supportable by
this literature.

### B2. Goldhammer, Naumann, Stelter, Tóth, Rölke & Klieme (2014) — CONTRADICTS.
*Abstract and body excerpts fetched via search-tool extraction of the pedocs OA PDF; the PDF itself
would not download for me (curl returned 000; jina timed out). Bibliographic record confirmed via
Crossref: doi **10.1037/a0034716**, Journal of Educational Psychology **106**(3), 608–626, Aug 2014;
Semantic Scholar citationCount 286.* **Label: partially verified — I did not read the full paper.**

"The Time on Task Effect in Reading and Problem Solving Is Moderated by Task Difficulty and Skill:
Insights From a Computer-Based Large Scale Assessment." N = 1,020, ages 16–65, German field test of
PIAAC, computer-based reading and problem-solving tasks, linear mixed models.

Quoted result: "in problem solving, which required controlled processing, the time on task effect was
**positive** and **increased with task difficulty**. In reading tasks, which required more routine
processing, the time on task effect was **negative** and the **more negative, the easier a task was**.
In problem solving, the positive time on task effect **decreased with increasing skill level**. In
reading, the negative time on task effect **increased with increasing skill level**. These
heterogeneous effects suggest that time on task has **no uniform interpretation** but is a function of
task difficulty and individual skill."

**This is the single most damaging finding for our formula.** The nearest well-studied process
variable to ours has no fixed sign. Its sign depends on whether the task is routine or controlled and
on the candidate's own skill. Our formula assumes a fixed positive sign for every candidate and every
brief.

### B3. Goldhammer, Hahnel, Kroehne & Zehner (2021) — QUALIFIES.
**UNVERIFIED (abstract only; the pedocs PDF would not download).**
"From byproduct to design factor: On validating the interpretation of process indicators based on log
data", *Large-scale Assessments in Education* 9.
https://www.pedocs.de/volltexte/2022/25005/pdf/LsAE_2021_9_Goldhammer_et_al_From_byproduct_to_design_factor_A.pdf

Abstract position, quoted: "the intended uses and interpretations of process indicators **require
validation**, which here means a theoretical and/or empirical justification that inferences about
(latent) attributes of the test-taker's work process are valid", and log data needs "contextualizing
information … in order to **reduce interpretative ambiguities** regarding the derived process
indicators."

Read plainly: the field's own methodological standard says a log-derived indicator is a *byproduct*
until someone validates it. AILX has not done that validation for `processSignal`. This source does
not say the signal is wrong; it says shipping it unvalidated is off-standard.

## C. BIAS: FAMILIARITY, MODE, LANGUAGE, EFFORT

### C1. Wise & Kong (2005) — QUALIFIES. *Full PDF fetched.*
"Response Time Effort: A New Measure of Examinee Motivation in Computer-Based Tests", *Applied
Measurement in Education* 18(2), 163–183.

Numbers: RTE coefficient alpha = **.97**. Splitting N = 472 examinees into RTE < .80, .80–.90, > .90:
- test performance M = 37.87 / 47.33 / **63.26**, F(2,469) = 87.22, p < .001, **ω² = .26**
- self-reported effort M = 12.50 / 13.33 / 16.38, F = 16.41, p < .001, ω² = .06
- **SAT-Verbal 554.00 / 574.00 / 575.77, F = 1.08, p = .36, ω² = .00**
- SAT-Quantitative 589.50 / 582.67 / 582.72 (n.s.)

**Two-edged for us, and honestly so.** (a) It supports the general idea that behavioural log data
carries real information about engagement — and that information is *not* ability (SAT effects are
zero). (b) It therefore says a process signal measures **motivation/effort**, a construct-irrelevant
variable when the target construct is AI-literacy skill. 26% of the variance in test performance was
explained by whether the examinee bothered. That is exactly the variance our 25 points would import
into the composite.

### C2. Akyol (2021) — QUALIFIES (with an honest negative result). *Full text fetched.*
"Comparison of Computer-based and Paper-based Exams: Evidence from PISA", *Boğaziçi Journal Review of
Social, Economic and Administrative Studies* 35(2), 137–150. doi:10.21773/boun.35.2.2

Difference-in-differences on the 2015 PISA mode switch (57 countries switched, 15 stayed on paper),
Turkey focus. Computer-based delivery reduced Turkish scores by **28.85 (math), 29.52 (science),
39.975 (reading)** points — "a 5.9 percent decrease in math and science, and 8.1 percent decrease in
reading". Covariates included computer possession, language spoken at home, immigration status, ESCS.

**Negative result, reported plainly:** "Our results **do not indicate heterogeneity in results by
computer possession**." The only heterogeneity found was gender (males worse in reading). So this
paper establishes that *the interface itself* moves scores by a large amount, but it **does not**
establish that computer-ownership/familiarity is the moderator. Anyone citing PISA to argue "tool
familiarity biases process signals" should know this paper looked and did not find it.

### C3. Liang, Yuksekgonul, Mao, Wu & Zou (2023) — CONTRADICTS (language bias). *Full PDF fetched.*
"GPT detectors are biased against non-native English writers", arXiv:2304.02819v3; published in
*Patterns* 4(7), 100779. https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.02819

Numbers: seven widely-used GPT detectors, 91 human-authored TOEFL essays vs 88 US 8th-grade ASAP
essays. "near-perfect accuracy for US 8-th grade essays. However, they misclassified over half of the
TOEFL essays as 'AI-generated' (**average false positive rate: 61.22%**)". All seven detectors
unanimously flagged **18/91 (19.78%)**; at least one detector flagged **89/91 (97.80%)**. The
unanimously flagged essays "had significantly **lower perplexity**" (p = 9.74E-05). Prompting GPT to
raise linguistic diversity removed the bias — and also let AI text bypass the detectors.

**Why this belongs in Q5.** It is the cleanest published demonstration that a *surface linguistic
feature* used as a proxy signal systematically penalises non-native English writers, at a very large
effect size. Our `distinctPrompts` dedup is a surface-text operation on candidate-written English.
Whatever dedup rule we use (exact match, edit distance, embedding similarity) will interact with
lexical range. A writer with a narrower lexicon produces prompts that look more like each other and
so dedups *down*. The direction of the bias is predictable and against non-native speakers. This is
an argument by mechanism, not a measurement of our formula.

### C4. Reusens, Borchert, De Weerdt & Baesens (2025) — QUALIFIES.
**UNVERIFIED (abstract only.)** "Native Design Bias: Studying the Impact of English Nativeness on
Language Model Performance", *Findings of IJCNLP-AACL 2025*. doi:10.18653/v1/2025.findings-ijcnlp.73

Abstract: 12,000+ annotations from 124 annotators with recorded native language and English
proficiency. "performance discrepancies occur when LLMs are prompted by the different groups for the
**classification tasks**. **Generative tasks, in contrast, are largely robust to nativeness bias**,
likely due to their longer context length". Also a "strong **anchoring effect** when the model is made
aware of the user's nativeness … regardless of the correctness of this information."

**Reported honestly, this partially cuts against the language-bias worry**: for *generative* tasks —
which is what T1 is — the authors found the model's output quality largely robust to prompter
nativeness. It does not speak to prompt *count* or *length*.

### C5. GAP — NO EVIDENCE EITHER WAY.
I searched (Exa, OpenAlex, arXiv, Crossref, Semantic Scholar) for published evidence that non-native
English speakers issue **more prompts**, **longer prompts**, or **more revision cycles** to an AI
assistant, or that typing speed mediates prompt count in AI-assisted work. **I found none.** The L2
keystroke-logging literature (e.g. Révész et al. 2019, *SSLA*, 30 advanced Chinese L2 users of
English, IELTS Task 2, keystroke + eye-tracking + stimulated recall — abstract only) establishes that
L2 writers' pausing and revision behaviour is structured and interpretable, but studies *writing*, not
*prompting*, and reports no group comparison against native writers on revision counts.

So: the claim "our process signal is biased against non-native speakers" is **plausible by mechanism
(C3) and unsupported by direct evidence.** Do not ship it as an evidenced claim. It is a
should-have-been-tested risk, and it is cheap to test on our own logs once we have them.

## D. VERBOSITY ≠ QUALITY (one-line adjacency, as asked)

### D1. Saito, Wachi, Wataoka & Akimoto (2023) — supports the verbosity worry. *Full PDF fetched.*
"Verbosity Bias in Preference Labeling by Large Language Models", arXiv:2310.10076.
Finding, quoted: "in our problem setting, **GPT-4 prefers longer answers more than humans**"; their
Figure 3 shows "a positive correlation between word count difference between the two options and the
resulting evaluation" across three writing prompts. They note humans in HH-RLHF also prefer longer
answers, so the bias is a *difference in degree*, not a pure artefact.

### D2. "Judging the Judges" (arXiv:2604.23178v2) — QUALIFIES D1.
**UNVERIFIED (abstract only.)** Length-aware measurement across five judge models: "Verbosity bias is
**heterogeneous** across models … Llama, Gemini Pro, and Gemini Flash show classical verbosity bias
(+0.24 to +0.44, prefer longer), **Claude Sonnet 4 shows the opposite (−0.12, prefer concise)**, and
GPT-4o is essentially neutral (−0.04)". Style bias (markdown vs prose) was larger (0.10–0.76) than
verbosity, and position bias was small (≤0.04).

**Take:** "LLM judges reward verbosity" is real but model-dependent and smaller than style bias.
State it as one qualified line, not a headline. Relevance to T1 is indirect — it concerns the *judge*
of the artefact, not `processSignal`, which is model-free.

## E. THE PERCEPTION INVERSION (why nobody notices the signal is wrong)

### E1. METR (2025) — CONTRADICTS. *Full blog post fetched.*
Becker et al., "Measuring the Impact of Early-2025 AI on Experienced Open-Source Developer
Productivity", 2025-07-10.
https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/

RCT: 16 experienced developers, their own repos (avg 22k+ stars, 1M+ LOC), **246 real issues**
randomised to AI-allowed vs AI-disallowed, ~2h per task, screen-recorded.
- "**they take 19% longer** to complete issues" with AI.
- "developers expected AI to speed them up by **24%**, and even **after experiencing the slowdown**,
  they still believed AI had sped them up by **20%**."
- METR published a follow-up on late-2025 tools in Feb 2026 (linked from the post; not fetched here).

**Relevance.** Heavy AI interaction volume was associated with *worse* real throughput, and the
practitioners themselves could not perceive it — a ~39 pp perception-reality gap. If expert
practitioners cannot tell that high AI interaction volume made them slower, an automatic scorer
counting prompts and cycles is not measuring productive skill either. This is the strongest
*contemporary, AI-specific* evidence against volume-as-signal.

## G. WHAT I DID NOT FIND

- **No study** scores or validates a prompt-count or revision-cycle metric in an AI-assisted
  assessment. There is no direct literature for or against our exact formula.
- **No study** links non-native English proficiency to prompt count/length with an AI assistant (§C5).
- **No study** links typing speed to AI-assisted task outcome in an assessment setting.
- The PISA computer-familiarity story is weaker than assumed: the one causal mode-effect paper I
  fetched found the mode effect large but **not moderated by computer possession** (§C2).

Stating this plainly: **the volume-monotone process signal is not contradicted by a direct study,
because no direct study exists. It is contradicted by every adjacent literature that has looked at a
structurally identical measure.**

## IF THE TEAM WANTS TO KEEP A PROCESS SIGNAL

Not a recommendation to keep it — a list of what the evidence above would require.

1. **Drop monotonicity.** B2 says the sign is not fixed. Any defensible term must be conditioned on
   task difficulty and candidate skill, which we cannot estimate from one T1 task.
2. **Require a link between prompt and artefact.** Every gaming attack in the verdict table works
   because prompts are counted, not traced. A cycle should only count if the artefact changed in a
   way the prompt asked for. That is a judge call, which puts a model back on the path — check this
   against the recompute invariant in AGENTS.md before proposing it.
3. **Cap the weight far below 25/160**, or move it out of the score entirely. B1's 48–82% person×task
   error on a *single* task is the hard number here.
4. **Use it as a flag, not a score.** C1 is the model: RTE detects non-engagement (ω² = .26 on
   performance, ω² = .00 on SAT-V) and is used to *filter*, not to award points.
5. **Run the missing DIF study on our own logs** (§C5, §G): prompt count and length by self-reported
   first language and by prior tool familiarity. Nobody has published it. We would be first, and it
   is cheap.
