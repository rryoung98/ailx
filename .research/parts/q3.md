# Q3 — Is there evidence for a RELATIONSHIP measure (did the prompt/revision IMPROVE the artefact) over a volume count?

**Status:** all sources below were FETCHED as full text unless explicitly marked UNVERIFIED.
Date of search: this session. Searched via Exa, OpenAlex, ACL Anthology, ERIC, ACM Queue, EDM proceedings.

---

## VERDICT

**Yes, the relationship measure is better — and no, AILX cannot compute it model-free.**

Three findings hold together, and they cut in opposite directions:

1. **A volume count is a KNOWN-BAD proxy in the nearest literature.** Faigley & Witte (1981),
   the founding revision taxonomy, found that EXPERT adult writers were the *least* frequent
   revisers (137 changes / 1000 words) and advanced students the *most* (236). A monotone
   volume score ranks the experts last. Forsgren et al. (2021) state the software analogue as
   a rule: "Activity metrics alone ... should never be used in isolation either to reward or to
   penalize developers."
2. **When you split revisions by TYPE, only the meaning-changing ones predict improvement.**
   Zhang & Litman (2015): partial correlation with Draft-2 score, controlling Draft-1 score,
   is r = 0.546 (p < 0.001) for text-based revisions and r = 0.137 (p = 0.363, n.s.) for
   surface revisions. The relationship measure genuinely carries the signal.
3. **But "did it improve the artefact" cannot be labelled reliably, let alone scored
   automatically.** Afrin & Litman (2018) asked 7 crowd annotators whether each of 940 essay
   revisions made the text better. **Fleiss kappa = 0.201 ("slight").** Restricting to items
   where 5 of 7 agreed still only reaches **0.263 ("fair")**. Their best automatic classifier
   scored **F1 = 0.551 against a 0.454 majority baseline** — and its top features included
   *length difference* (predicted-Better mean +4.81 chars/tokens vs -3.99 for NotBetter), i.e.
   the model itself fell back on volume.

**The one thing that IS model-free and does work:** Bosu, Greiler & Bird (2015) found that a
code review comment which *triggered a code change within one line of the highlighted lines*
predicted human-judged usefulness at **88% precision / 78% recall**. That is a diff-linkage,
not a semantic judgement — computable with no model. It measures "this act changed the
artefact", NOT "this act improved it".

**Recommendation implied by the evidence.** If AILX wants a relationship term in `processSignal`
without a judge on the recompute path, the defensible operationalisation is a *coupling*
measure — the prompt/response was followed by a change to the artefact within a bounded window
— not a *quality* measure. Anything that claims to score "productive" prompting model-free is
claiming a labelling reliability (kappa ~0.2 for humans) that nobody in the published record has.

## SOURCE-BY-SOURCE

### 1. Faigley, L. & Witte, S. (1981). *Analyzing Revision.* College Composition and Communication 32(4), 400–414. — **CONTRADICTS volume-monotone scoring**
- DOI 10.2307/356602 (390 citations, OpenAlex). Full text fetched from the ERIC companion
  report ED200978, *Using Text Structure Models for Analyzing Revision*:
  https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED200978.pdf
- Design: 18 case studies — 6 inexperienced student writers, 6 advanced student writers,
  6 expert adult writers. Taxonomy splits SURFACE (formal + meaning-preserving) from
  TEXT-BASE (microstructure + macrostructure) changes.
- **Exact numbers (fetched):** experts made a change on average **137 times per 1000 words**;
  inexperienced students **173**; advanced students **236**. "The advanced students were the
  most frequent revisers of the three groups." Only **12%** of inexperienced writers' revisions
  were TEXT-BASE; for expert adults it was about **35%**.
- Why it matters: the *rate* of revision is inversely ordered against expertise here. The
  *composition* (fraction meaning-changing) is what separates experts. n = 18 is tiny and this
  is descriptive case-study work, not inferential — but it is the source the whole downstream
  literature builds on, and it directly contradicts "more revisions = better".

### 2. Zhang, F. & Litman, D. (2015). *Annotation and Classification of Argumentative Writing Revisions.* BEA @ NAACL, W15-0616. — **QUALIFIES (type beats volume) / CONTRADICTS (automation is weak)**
- https://aclanthology.org/W15-0616.pdf — full text fetched.
- Corpora: C1 (high-school AP, expert-scored drafts, N = 1262 revisions), C2 (38 students, 495
  revisions, no expert scores).
- **Human reliability of the TAXONOMY (kappa, per category, C1 / C2):** Organization 1 / 1,
  Conventions 0.74 / 0.87, Word-usage 1 / 1, Claim 0.76 / 0.89, Warrant 0.78 / 0.85,
  Rebuttal 1 / 1, General Content 0.76 / 0.80, Evidence 1 / 1. Sentence alignment kappa 0.794.
  → *Revision PURPOSE is reliably codable by humans.*
- **Predicting improvement (partial correlation with Draft-2 score, Draft-1 regressed out, C1):**
  | measure | R | p |
  |---|---|---|
  | # all revisions | 0.516 | <0.001 |
  | # surface revisions | 0.137 | 0.363 (n.s.) |
  | — Organization | 0.201 | 0.180 |
  | — Conventions | −0.041 | 0.778 |
  | — Word-usage/Clarity | 0.135 | 0.371 |
  | # text-based revisions | **0.546** | <0.001 |
  | — Claim/Ideas | 0.472 | 0.001 |
  | — Warrant/Reasoning | 0.462 | 0.001 |
  | — Evidence | 0.415 | 0.004 |
  | — General Content | 0.259 | 0.083 |
- **Honest caveat for AILX: raw volume was NOT null here.** # all revisions correlated
  R = 0.516. So a volume count is not worthless in this domain — it is just *dominated* by the
  meaning-changing subset, and it is contaminated by a surface component with r ≈ 0.14.
- **Automated classification (the part that breaks AILX's model-free constraint):**
  surface-vs-text-based on C1, 10-fold CV, unweighted **F = 55.30** with all features vs
  majority baseline **37.12** and unigram **46.69**. Fine-grained per-purpose F-scores 49–76.
  End-to-end PIPELINE (extract then classify): **precision 40.25, recall 45.05** on C1;
  48.08 / 54.30 on C2 — "significantly drop compared to Tables 6 and 7".
- Extrinsic check: with *predicted* labels, text-based still correlates 0.542–0.553 and surface
  stays non-significant (0.175, p = 0.245). So an imperfect classifier still preserves the
  aggregate relationship — a real point in favour of the approach at cohort level, not at the
  level of one candidate's 25 points.

### 3. Afrin, T. & Litman, D. (2018). *Annotation and Classification of Sentence-level Revision Improvement.* BEA @ NAACL, W18-0528, pp. 240–246. — **CONTRADICTS a model-free improvement score. THE KEY RELIABILITY NUMBER.**
- https://aclanthology.org/W18-0528.pdf — full text fetched.
- This is the paper that asks exactly Q3's question: given (original sentence, revised
  sentence), is the revision **Better** or **NotBetter**?
- 940 ArgRewrite revisions, **7 Amazon Mechanical Turk annotators each**:
  | subset | #revisions | #Better | #NotBetter | Fleiss kappa |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | All | 940 (100%) | 784 (83.4%) | 156 (16.6%) | **0.201 (slight)** |
  | majority ≥ 5 of 7 | 748 (79.6%) | 658 (88.0%) | 90 (12.0%) | **0.263 (fair)** |
  The authors' own comparison anchor: Tan & Lee (2014) got Fleiss kappa 0.242 with 9 annotators
  on statement strength.
- **Classifier (Random Forest, 10-fold CV, unweighted P/R/F1):** majority baseline 0.417 /
  0.500 / 0.454; ArgRewrite-only 0.570 / 0.534 / 0.525; best (ArgRewrite + AESW plaintext)
  **0.574 / 0.555 / 0.551**. Recall on the NotBetter class is explicitly called "very low".
- **The most damaging detail for a volume-monotone design:** length difference was a top-5
  feature in every experiment, and for the best model the mean length difference of revisions
  predicted **Better** was **+4.81** vs **−3.99** for NotBetter. The authors write: "our models
  might similarly rely too heavily on length and classify longer sentences as Better."
  A quality classifier trained on this data partly re-learns the volume heuristic AILX is
  trying to escape.
- The authors' own conclusion: they want to "improve inter-rater reliability by collecting
  expert annotations rather than using crowdsourcing" — i.e. they treat kappa 0.20 as a defect.

### 4. Afrin, T. & Litman, D. (2023). *Predicting Desirable Revisions of Evidence and Reasoning in Argumentative Writing.* Findings of EACL 2023, pp. 2550–2561. — **QUALIFIES: the relationship measure works, but only with expert coders + context + feedback**
- https://aclanthology.org/2023.findings-eacl.193.pdf — full text fetched.
- "Desirable revisions (e.g., add relevant evidence) are student revisions that have
  hypothesized utility in improving an essay in response to feedback; undesirable revisions
  (e.g., add irrelevant evidence) do not."
- **Reliability with EXPERT coders, not crowd:** revision-purpose annotation Cohen kappa
  **0.753**; the evidence/reasoning scheme kappa **0.833 (evidence)** and **0.719 (reasoning)**;
  the essay improvement grade (0–3) kappa **0.77**. Contrast with 0.201 in #3 — the difference
  is expert coders working with a code book, essay context, and the feedback the student
  received. That is exactly the apparatus AILX does not have at scoring time.
- **Classification F1 (10-fold CV, augmented):** baseline LogR 0.462–0.537; best model
  (context + feedback) 0.521 (high-school evidence) to 0.679 (high-school reasoning). N of
  students is tiny: elementary 143, high-school 47, college 60.
- **Extrinsic (Pearson r of revision counts vs improvement score), GOLD labels:**
  desirable evidence 0.200* (elementary), desirable reasoning 0.450* (elementary),
  desirable evidence 0.391* (high-school), desirable reasoning 0.351* (high-school),
  college reasoning 0.029 (n.s.). **Undesirable counts were non-significant everywhere**
  (0.039, −0.022, 0.040, 0.272, −0.131). *p < .05.
- Read carefully: counting *desirable* revisions correlates with improvement at r ≈ 0.2–0.45,
  and counting *undesirable* ones tells you nothing. That is the strongest published support
  for "quality-filtered count beats raw count". It also failed entirely on the college cohort.

### 5. Bosu, A., Greiler, M. & Bird, C. (2015). *Characteristics of Useful Code Reviews: An Empirical Study at Microsoft.* MSR 2015. — **SUPPORTS an outcome-LINKED (not quality-judged) signal; the best code analogue found**
- https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/bosu2015useful.pdf — full text fetched.
- 1.5 million review comments across five Microsoft projects; oracle built from developer
  interviews plus manual coding of 844 comments.
- **Inter-rater reliability: Fleiss kappa = 0.947** ("almost perfect"), 3 raters, 100 comments,
  disagreeing on 3. Note WHY this is so much higher than #3: the question is "was this comment
  useful to you", asked of *the developer who received it*, with a code book — not a third
  party guessing whether text got better.
- **The model-free signal:** "If a comment triggered changes within one line distance to
  comment-highlighted lines, it was highly likely to be useful (**precision 88%, recall 78%**)."
  Change-triggering was one of the two most important model features.
- Full classifier (decision tree, 100 × 10-fold CV): **mean precision 89.1%, mean recall 85.1%,
  mean classification error 16.6%.** Held-out check against author ratings: 86.7% precision,
  93.8% recall on 125 comments.
- **Volume cuts the wrong way here too:** "the more files that are in a change, the lower the
  proportion of comments in the code review that will be of value"; comment threads with only
  one comment/participant were useful 88% of the time vs 51% for multi-comment threads.
  More activity in a thread predicted LESS usefulness.

### 6. Forsgren, N., Storey, M-A., Maddila, C., Zimmermann, T., Houck, B. & Butler, J. (2021). *The SPACE of Developer Productivity.* ACM Queue 19(1). — **CONTRADICTS volume-monotone scoring, as an explicit norm**
- https://queue.acm.org/detail.cfm?id=3454124 — full text fetched.
- Exact quote, from "Myth: Productivity is all about developer activity": "Activity metrics
  alone do not reveal which of these is the case, so they should never be used in isolation
  either to reward or to penalize developers."
- Also: "higher volumes of activity appear for various reasons: working longer hours may signal
  developers having to 'brute-force' work to overcome bad systems or poor planning"; and
  activity counts of "pull requests, commits, or code reviews are prone to errors because of
  gaps in data and measurement errors."
- This is a position/framework paper, not an empirical study. It carries normative weight in
  industry, no effect sizes.

### 7. Gong, Y. & Beck, J. E. (2015). *Towards Detecting Wheel-Spinning: Future Failure in Mastery Learning.* / EDM 2015 paper 84. — **CONTRADICTS volume-monotone scoring (ITS analogue)**
- https://www.educationaldatamining.org/EDM2015/uploads/papers/paper_84.pdf — full text fetched.
- Definition (from Beck & Gong 2013): a student who takes **10 practice opportunities without
  mastering a skill** is "wheel spinning" — "despite rapid movement of the wheels, the car is
  going nowhere."
- **Prevalence, as reported in this fetched paper citing Beck & Gong 2013: ~31% of
  student-skill pairs in Cognitive Tutor and ~38% in ASSISTments are wheel spinning.**
  In their own ASSISTments data (7591 students, 31301 train / 16955 test student-skill pairs):
  20.6% train / 19.2% test wheel spinning.
- Prerequisite gradient: bottom 20% of prerequisite knowledge wheel-spin **50%** of the time;
  top 20% only **10%**. Detection model Cox-Snell R² 0.264 → 0.268, AUC 0.884 → 0.888 with the
  prerequisite feature added.
- Direct relevance: in a system that counts *attempts*, roughly a fifth to nearly two-fifths of
  effort is by construction unproductive. A monotone-in-attempts score pays for it.

### 8. Sommers, N. (1980). *Revision Strategies of Student Writers and Experienced Adult Writers.* CCC 31(4). — **UNVERIFIED (metadata only)**
- Existence verified via OpenAlex: DOI 10.2307/356588, 753 citations. **I did not fetch the
  full text**, so I make no claim about its contents beyond its existence. It is the other
  canonical revision paper alongside Faigley & Witte; cite it only after fetching.

### 9. Kashefi, O., Afrin, T., Dale, M., Olshefski, C., Godley, A., Litman, D. & Hwa, R. (2022). *ArgRewrite V.2: an annotated argumentative revisions corpus.* Language Resources and Evaluation. — **UNVERIFIED (abstract only)**
- DOI 10.1007/s10579-021-09567-z. Springer full text is paywalled; the fetch returned metadata
  and the reference list only. Abstract confirms: two revision cycles on self-driving-car
  essays, annotations at coarse and fine purpose granularity and at sentential and
  subsentential scope, plus revision goals, essay scores and annotation verification. **I could
  not extract its agreement statistics.** If precise ArgRewrite V.2 kappas are needed, get the
  PDF through an institutional copy.

### 10. Fitzgerald, J. (1987). *Research on Revision in Writing.* Review of Educational Research 57(4), 481–506. — **UNVERIFIED (abstract only)**
- DOI 10.3102/00346543057004481. The canonical review. Abstract emphasises the field's shift to
  "meaningful revision activity, not just editorial actions" — consistent with the verdict, but
  I did not fetch the body and quote no findings from it.

### 11. GitClear (2024/2025). *Coding on Copilot / AI Copilot Code Quality.* — **WEAK, industry, non-peer-reviewed**
- https://www.gitclear.com/coding_on_copilot_data_shows_ais_downward_pressure_on_code_quality
- Claims from ~153M (2024 report) / 211M (2025 report) changed lines: code churn — "the
  percentage of lines that are reverted or updated less than two weeks after being authored" —
  projected to double in 2024 vs the 2021 pre-AI baseline; 4x growth in code cloning.
- Only read at the landing/summary level. Vendor research with a commercial interest in the
  conclusion, no peer review, no released data. **Do not use as load-bearing evidence.** It is
  worth noting only because "churn" is another example of the same idea as #5: an edit that
  is undone shortly after is a *measurable* negative outcome linkage.

## WHAT WAS NOT FOUND — reportable negatives

- **No study operationalises "productive PROMPT" the way AILX would need.** Searches for prompt
  count vs output quality returned only low-quality or unverifiable hits (a Zenodo preprint and
  an arXiv HTML page with implausible dates), which I have deliberately excluded rather than
  cite. There is no published analogue of "this prompt improved the artefact, that one did not"
  with reliability statistics.
- **No published measure of code-edit usefulness that runs without either (a) a human judgement
  or (b) an outcome link (change-trigger, revert, churn).** The only reliable automated signals
  in the code literature are outcome links.
- **No evidence either way on whether a volume-monotone process score can be gamed by a
  candidate who knows the rule.** That specific threat model is unstudied in the sources found.

## PRACTICAL SUMMARY FOR THE 25-POINT `processSignal`

| candidate design | published support | reliability available |
|---|---|---|
| count distinct prompts, count cycles (current) | Zhang & Litman r = 0.516 for raw count | perfect (deterministic), but Faigley & Witte inverts it against expertise, SPACE forbids it in isolation |
| classify each revision surface vs meaning-changing | Zhang & Litman r = 0.546 vs 0.137 | pipeline P 40 / R 45 — needs a model |
| label each revision Better / NotBetter | Afrin & Litman | **human Fleiss kappa 0.201**; best model F1 0.551. Not viable. |
| label desirable vs undesirable with expert code book + context + feedback | Afrin & Litman 2023, gold r = 0.20–0.45 | expert kappa 0.72–0.83 — needs experts, not a scorer |
| **link the prompt to a subsequent artefact change (coupling, not quality)** | **Bosu et al.: P 88 / R 78 for change-trigger → usefulness** | **deterministic from the diff — no model on the recompute path** |

The last row is the only one that is both evidence-backed and compatible with a pure,
byte-identically recomputable `score()`.
