# Q1 — Does any published work SCORE the process of human–AI collaboration and VALIDATE it against an outcome?

Prepared for TEN-80 (Foray Track 1 `processSignal`). All sources below were FETCHED in full text unless
explicitly marked UNVERIFIED (abstract only).

## VERDICT

**Almost nothing validates a process score against an independent outcome, and the handful of studies
that measure VOLUME against an outcome disagree with each other.**

Three sharper claims the evidence supports:

1. **There is no published, validated, volume-monotone process score.** No paper found builds a scored
   instrument of the form "count distinct prompts, count revision cycles, award points" and then shows
   that score has criterion validity. The closest things are (a) research *variables* (prompt counts)
   entered into a regression, and (b) *rubrics* that were validated for structure and inter-rater
   agreement but never against an outcome.
2. **Volume-vs-outcome results are contradictory and sign-unstable across settings.** Prompt count
   positively predicts performance in two education studies (Yang 2025, β=0.256; Kim/Lee 2025,
   β=0.206–0.754). Dialogue-turn count is essentially zero or strongly negative against team outcomes
   in a co-design study (Jiang 2025, NDT–TP r=−0.01, NDT–PP r=−0.92). One agent-loop benchmark finds
   iteration slightly *hurts* quality. So "more is better" is not a settled empirical fact.
3. **Where prompt count DOES predict, it is heavily confounded with prompt QUALITY.** In Yang et al.
   prompt number correlates r=0.747 with Depth-of-Knowledge and r=0.738 with prompt relevance. Volume
   is acting as a *proxy* for quality in an ungamed setting. A volume-monotone score in a
   *stakes-bearing exam* removes exactly the condition (no incentive to inflate) that made the proxy
   work. No paper found tests the proxy under an incentive to inflate it. **That gap is the single
   most decision-relevant finding for TEN-80.**
4. **The revision-cycle half of the Foray formula has direct disconfirming evidence.** In the only
   study found that generated artefacts before AND after a revision cycle and rated both
   (Oppenlaender et al. 2024, n=50), one revision improved only ~1/3 of image sets; ~50% were
   unchanged and 15% got *worse*. Counting cycles counted an event that improved the artefact a
   minority of the time.

Secondary finding worth stating plainly: **the AI-literacy measurement field agrees self-report is
inadequate and is moving to performance-based tests — but it is moving to KNOWLEDGE tests (GLAT,
AICOS) and to descriptive log ANALYSIS (ENA, process mining), not to scored process instruments.**
The scored-process-with-criterion-validity cell of the matrix is empty.

## A. Studies that relate PROCESS VOLUME to an outcome

### A1. Yang, Jiang, Li, Herman, Luo, Chappell Moots & Lovett (2025) — SUPPORTS (with a large caveat)
*British Journal of Educational Technology* 56(5), doi:10.1111/bjet.13588.
Full text fetched from the ODU institutional repository copy.

(a) **What was scored.** 73 nontraditional undergraduate/graduate students' ChatGPT chat logs across
multiple courses, coded on four log-derived metrics: **prompt number** (raw count), **Depth of
Knowledge** (Webb's DoK, 1–4), **prompt relevance/evolution** (1–4), **originality** (1–4). Outcome =
instructor-graded course artefact performance (percentage score, M=67.76, SD=18.34).

(b) **Validation.** Multiple regression, performance ~ prompt number + engagement + self-efficacy:
R²=0.17, F(3,69)=4.70, p<0.01. **Prompt number β=0.256, p=0.03**; engagement β=0.267, p=0.05;
self-efficacy β=0.086, p=0.51 (n.s.). Pearson correlations (Bonferroni-adjusted α=0.02):
DoK r=0.40, prompt relevance r=0.42, **originality r=−0.13 (n.s., negative)**. Text-mining clusters
differed on performance, F(3,78)=3.451, p=0.02; "strategic inquirers" (Cluster 1) beat Cluster 3
(Tukey p=0.025).

(c) **Volume-monotone?** Yes — prompt number is exactly the Foray `distinctPrompts` term, and it is
the ONLY log metric that survived into the regression.

**Caveat that matters more than the result.** Prompt number correlates **r=0.747 with DoK** and
**r=0.738 with prompt relevance** (both p<0.003). Mean prompt number was 7.03 (SD 7.04, range 1–34) in
a low-stakes coursework setting with no reward for prompting more. The count is a proxy for
questioning quality, not an independent causal lever. Also note "originality of work" was
*negatively* correlated with DoK (−0.291) and relevance (−0.277) — more AI-interaction depth, less
original work.

### A2. Choi, Lee, Han & Han (2025), "Effects of Prompt Elements on Problem-Solving Performance and User Experience" — SUPPORTS
*SAGE Open*, doi:10.1177/21582440251381680. Full text fetched.

(a) **What was scored.** Logs from a ChatGPT prompting *contest* at a South Korean university (May
2023, n≈62 undergraduates), two open-ended creative-problem-solving tasks (trip report; fairy tale).
Five prompt features coded: contextual assignment, **number of prompts**, assigned persona, output
format designation, critical attitude. Outcome = expert-rated CPS performance score.

(b) **Validation.** Hierarchical regression. Task 1: Model 1 (quality features only) R²=0.505; adding
**number of prompts** β=0.206, p<0.01, ΔR²=0.031. Task 2: Model 1 R²=0.473; adding number of prompts
**β=0.754, p<0.01, ΔR²=0.164**; zero-order r(prompt count, score)=**0.763**. Context (β=1.162 / 1.190)
and critical attitude (β=2.392 / 2.412) were the largest predictors in both tasks.

(c) **Volume-monotone?** Yes, and it adds *incremental* variance beyond quality features — the
strongest evidence found FOR the Foray design.

**Caveat.** It is a contest. Participants were explicitly competing on output quality, so prompt count
measures persistence at a goal the scorer also measured. Prompt count is correlated r=0.501 with
context and r=0.312 with persona — again confounded with quality. n≈62, single institution, GPT-3.5 era.

### A3. Jiang, Chen, Li, Liu & Clarkson (2025), "AI-Augmented Co-Design in Healthcare" — CONTRADICTS
*Behavioral Sciences* 15(12), 1704, doi:10.3390/bs15121704. Full text fetched.

(a) **What was scored.** Six four-person teams (24 participants) in an AI-facilitated co-design
session. Log-derived process markers: **Number of Dialogue Turns (NDT)**, Total Dialogue Duration
(TDD), Average Turn Duration (ATD), Turn-Taking Balance (TTB), role-specific turn counts. Outcomes:
expert-rated Technical Performance (TP, 5-item rubric, ICC(2,k) reported), Perceived Performance (PP),
Self-rated Technical Performance (S-TP).

(b) **Validation.** Pearson r with percentile-bootstrap CIs (B=10,000), team level, n=6 teams,
descriptive only, no NHST. **NDT–PP r=−0.922 (95% CI −1.000 to −0.729)**; Spearman ρ=−0.928.
TDD–S-TP r=−0.750 (CI −0.998 to −0.168), ρ=−0.771. **NDT–TP r=−0.014 (CI −0.984 to 0.875)**.
ATD–PP r=0.570 (CI crosses 0); TTB–PP r=0.377 (CI crosses 0).

(c) **Volume-monotone?** The authors explicitly reject it: *"Teams with more balanced participation
tended to report higher perceived performance, whereas simply having more turns did not."* Their v0.4
design recommendation is turn-quality scaffolds "rather than free-form chatter."

**Caveat.** n=6 teams. Confidence intervals are enormous and the paper says so. Outcomes are partly
self-report (PP, S-TP); the one expert-rated outcome (TP) gave r≈−0.01, i.e. no relationship at all.
This is a weak study statistically but it is the *only* one found that measured turn count against an
expert-rated artefact outcome, and the answer was "nothing."

### A4. Heilman, Kyllo & Murphy-Hill (2026), "GitHub Copilot and Developer Productivity: An Observational Dose-Response Analysis" — QUALIFIES (boundary case)
arXiv:2606.00438. **UNVERIFIED (abstract + search highlights only; full text not fetched).**

Tool-usage intensity, not a candidate process score. 16,223 Microsoft engineers, 43 weeks, engineer
fixed effects + PPML two-way FE holding active coding time constant. Engineers complete **40.5% more
PRs** in their highest-Copilot weeks vs zero-usage weeks; "the gradient is monotonic with diminishing
returns at high intensity." Seven robustness/falsification tests. Relevant only as: in an *unincentivised
observational* setting, AI-usage volume is monotone-with-saturation against a real outcome — the same
shape Foray assumes. It says nothing about a scored exam, and the authors' own concern (heavy weeks are
busy weeks) is the confound analogue of exam gaming.

## B. The revision-cycle assumption, tested directly

### B1. Oppenlaender, Linder & Silvennoinen (2024/2025), "Prompting AI Art: An Investigation into the Creative Skill of Prompt Engineering" — CONTRADICTS
*International Journal of Human-Computer Interaction*, doi:10.1080/10447318.2024.2431761. Full text fetched.

(a) **What was scored.** Three crowdsourced studies, 227 participants total. Study 3 (n=50) is the
relevant one: participants saw 5 images generated from each of their own three prompts, then were
*instructed to improve* those prompts. Deterministic Latent Diffusion, fixed seed 1040790415, so the
two image sets are directly comparable. Prompts compared by POS-tagged token add/remove and Levenshtein
distance; images compared on details, contrast, colour, distortions, watermarks, consistency.

(b) **Validation.** This IS the outcome comparison. Mean Levenshtein distance between prompt pairs
= 28.1 (SD 25.0); 538 tokens added vs 243 removed. Result: **"over half of the revised sets showed no
improvement in image quality... About half of the sets remained the same, 15% were worse, and a third
were better."** 11 prompts (7.33%) were not changed at all, 6 of which were random pasted text.
Only 1 of 50 participants used community-standard prompt modifiers in all three prompts, even when
explicitly told to make artworks.

(c) **Volume-monotone?** No — the study is the direct refutation of one. A revision cycle happened for
~93% of prompts; it improved the artefact for ~33%. **A `cycles` counter would have awarded full credit
to the 15% who made things worse and to the 6 people who pasted random text.**

Study 1 finding worth carrying: participants *could* discern prompt quality (ability increased with art
experience/interest) even though they could not *produce* good prompts. Judgement and production come
apart.

### B2. Shrivastava (2026), "Semantic Early-Stopping for Iterative LLM Agent Loops" — CONTRADICTS (weakly, different population)
arXiv:2606.27009. **UNVERIFIED (search highlights only).**

Agent loops, not humans. On HotpotQA: *"Iteration does not improve measured quality here — it slightly
hurts it."* The best policy is `fixed_k1`, return the first grounded draft: −86% tokens and the highest
quality (IS = 0.700, Δ = +0.030, TOST non-inferior). An oracle that picks the best round reaches 0.785
(+0.115, p≈4e−11), so a better round exists but no online signal finds it. The author's own limitation:
HotpotQA "underexercises iteration." Cite only as: iteration count is not self-evidently monotone in
quality, even for machines, and *identifying which round was the good one* is an open problem.

### B3. Bashardoust, Feng, Geissler, Feuerriegel & Shrestha (2024/2026), prompt-engineering training for journalists — QUALIFIES
arXiv:2409.12320. Full text fetched.

N=29 professional journalists wrote social-media posts about scientific articles with ChatGPT-3.5
before and after in-person prompt-engineering training. Training significantly raised **perceived**
expertise (3.38→3.72, Hedges' g=−0.39; β=0.357, p<0.05) and increased use of taught techniques,
especially in-context prompting and **iterative refinement**. But perceived helpfulness *fell*
(4.93→4.76, β=−0.286, n.s.), expert perception of output varied by task difficulty, and reader
perception was mixed across quality dimensions. In the appendix regressions, **iterative refinement
was NEGATIVELY associated with both perceived expertise and perceived helpfulness** (both p>0.05,
i.e. not significant — do not overstate).
Takeaway for TEN-80: training people to iterate more did not cleanly improve rated output quality.

## C. Instruments that SCORE the process but were never validated against an outcome

### C1. Li, Yan, Su, Shen & Mao (2025), "An Assessment of Human–AI Interaction Capability in the Generative AI Era" — QUALIFIES
*Journal of Intelligence* 13(6), 62, doi:10.3390/jintelligence13060062. Fetched (abstract + intro read
in full; results section only partly retrieved — treat the numeric detail below as abstract-level).

(a) **What was scored.** Explicitly "the behavioral process of human–GenAI interactions": interaction
capability operationalised into behavioural indicators and **rubrics**, applied to the documented
interaction processes of 121 undergraduates doing two real-world tasks with GenAI. This is the closest
published analogue to Foray's `processSignal` — a rubric scored on the interaction log itself.

(b) **Validation.** Reliability and *structural* validity only: acceptable inter-rater and internal
consistency reliability; EFA + CFA confirm a three-dimensional structure. Scores differed by gender,
academic background, AIGC use frequency, critical-thinking disposition, and "question chain logic."
**There is no criterion validation against task output quality.** High critical-thinking disposition
offset low usage frequency.

(c) **Volume-monotone?** No. The construct is dimensional (rubric-scored), not count-based. The one
volume-ish variable (GenAI usage frequency) is treated as a *covariate* that critical thinking can
substitute for — i.e. volume is explicitly not sufficient.

### C2. AI Prompt Writing Rubric (TOJET, v25 i2) — CONTRADICTS the "a rubric will be reliable" hope
*Turkish Online Journal of Educational Technology* 25(2), https://www.tojet.net/articles/v25i2/2522.pdf.
Full text fetched.

(a) **What was scored.** An analytic rubric for sixth-graders' prompt writing, built from a literature
review plus 7 experts, piloted on 32 students doing a poetry-writing task with ChatGPT 3.5.

(b) **Validation.** Content validity re-check, EFA, Cronbach's α = **.89** (7-item Factor 1) and **.76**
(2-item Factor 2). Four items dropped for low common variance; item 10 (politeness) dropped for
inverse loading. **Inter-rater agreement: Fleiss' κ = 0.29** across three raters — "fair to moderate,"
which by any standard is poor for a scored instrument. **No outcome validation at all.**

(c) **Volume-monotone?** No. But the κ=0.29 is the load-bearing number: even a purpose-built,
expert-reviewed prompt rubric applied by three trained raters to 32 children barely agreed with itself.
The paper also reports that Kobara et al. (2024) built K-12 AI-education rubrics with internal
consistency but "poor" inter-rater reliability. Judging prompt quality by rubric is genuinely hard.
*(This is the honest counterweight to "just replace the counter with a rubric.")*

### C3. Prompt Engineering Competence Scale (PECS) (2025) — NOT RELEVANT (self-report)
*Management Decision / SAGE*, doi:10.1177/02666669251336455. **UNVERIFIED (abstract + method excerpt only).**
437 users, 5-point Likert **self-report**, one-factor, Cronbach's α = 0.92, CFA good fit, item
discrimination indices computed. Zero behavioural data, zero outcome validation. Listed here because it
is what "prompt-engineering assessment" usually means in the literature — a questionnaire.

### C4. PEEM (2026), prompt/response rubric — QUALIFIES (machine prompts, not human process)
arXiv:2603.10477. Fetched (abstract + intro + results summary from the HTML; not a full read of tables).
9-axis LLM-judge rubric: 3 prompt criteria (clarity/structure, linguistic quality, fairness) + 6 response
criteria. Across 7 benchmarks × 5 task models, PEEM's accuracy axis tracks conventional accuracy at
Spearman ρ≈0.97, Pearson r≈0.94, p<0.001; multi-evaluator pairwise ρ=0.68–0.85; robustness rate
76.7–80.6% under meaning-preserving paraphrase; a PEEM-feedback rewriting loop improves downstream
accuracy by up to 11.7 points. **This is the one place a prompt-QUALITY score is validated against a
downstream outcome — but the prompts are model-generated benchmark prompts, and the "outcome" is
benchmark accuracy, not a human artefact.** It shows prompt quality is scorable and predictive in a
machine setting. It does not transfer to scoring a human candidate.

## D. Log analysis of human–AI dialogue that stops short of a score

### D1. Chen & Jia (2026), "Tracing GenAI Literacy: ... through Epistemic Network Analysis" — QUALIFIES
arXiv:2606.00040 (workshop paper, Peking University). Full text fetched.

(a) **What was scored.** Interaction logs from 162 university students on a GenAI-assisted abstract-
writing task (DeepSeek-backed custom platform, 30-word prompt limit, copy-paste disabled). Prompts
coded into 6 communicative acts: Abstract Generation Command, AI Improvement Command, Meta-Command,
Clarification Question, Factual Question, Challenging Question. Cohen's κ = 0.75 between two coders.
Epistemic Network Analysis models code CO-OCCURRENCE, not frequency.

(b) **Validation.** Against a validated **GenAI Literacy Test** (GLAT, ref [3]), median-split into High
(n=89) / Low (n=73). Mann-Whitney U on the primary ENA dimension: **U=683.50, p<.001, effect size .35**.
**There is no artefact-quality or grade outcome** — the "outcome" is another test score.

(c) **Volume-monotone?** Explicitly not: *"Unlike frequency counts, ENA models the co-occurrence of
codes."* The distinguishing signature is *structural*: high-literacy students link AI Improvement
Commands ↔ Clarification Questions ↔ Meta-Commands (an iterative sense-making→refinement cycle);
low-literacy students link Generation Commands ↔ Factual Questions (transactional). Note that the
low-literacy pattern **includes** Challenging Questions, but "as reactions to confusion or
hallucinations rather than strategic critique" — the same countable act meaning opposite things.

**This is the most directly transferable positive finding for Foray:** what separated competent from
incompetent users was *which acts co-occurred*, not how many acts there were.

### D2. Fan, Tang, Le, Kirschner, Wen, Zhou, Ou, Amiel, Lin, Li, Chen, Zhang, Zhao, Zhao, Gašević & Martinez-Maldonado (2024/2025), "Beware of metacognitive laziness" — QUALIFIES
*British Journal of Educational Technology* 56(2), doi:10.1111/bjet.13544. Full text fetched.

Randomised experiment, 117 university students, four conditions (ChatGPT / human expert / writing
analytics tool / no tool) on a writing task, with multi-channel process data coded into
self-regulated-learning processes and analysed for frequency AND sequence.
Findings: (1) no difference in post-task intrinsic motivation; (2) **significant differences in the
frequency and sequences of SRL processes across groups**; (3) the **ChatGPT group outperformed on essay
score improvement but knowledge gain and transfer were NOT significantly different**. The authors name
the mechanism "metacognitive laziness" — offloading regulation to the AI.

**Why this matters to TEN-80:** it is a clean demonstration that the *artefact* can improve while the
*person's* competence does not. A process signal that rewards interaction volume is at risk of scoring
the very offloading behaviour this paper flags. Process was measured and outcomes were measured, but the
process measures were never combined into a score or validated as a predictor.

### D3. "Uncovering Self-Regulated Learning Patterns in Chatbot-Powered Environment Through a Process Mining Lens" (AAEE 2025) — NO EVIDENCE EITHER WAY
https://aaee2025.org/microsite/pdf/full-paper_182.pdf. **UNVERIFIED (abstract only).**
Prompt-to-SRL schema (Pintrich's 4 phases, 8 actions) + process mining on a real chat dataset.
Reports non-sequential patterns: learning without planning, ending without reflection, phase skipping,
**repetition of phases**. Purely descriptive; **no outcome, no score.** Its warning is relevant though:
it cautions against chatbots that force learners through a canonical SRL sequence, because that
"may impose unnecessary learning burdens." An exam that pays for cycles imposes exactly such a burden.

## E. The self-report distinction (asked for explicitly)

The AI-literacy scale literature is dominated by self-report and its own authors now say so.

- **GLAT — Jin, Martínez-Maldonado, Gašević & Yan (2025)**, *Computers and Education: AI* 9, 100436;
  preprint arXiv:2411.00283 (full PDF fetched). **SUPPORTS performance-based measurement, not process
  scoring.** 20-item multiple-choice knowledge test; 355 higher-ed students; 2PL IRT model,
  Cronbach's α=0.80, ω_total=0.81, RMSEA=0.03, CFI=0.97. External validity (n=83 students, GenAI-chatbot
  visual-analytics task): model F(4,78)=9.233, p<.001, **R²=0.321**. **GLAT β=0.220, t=2.093, p=.040**;
  visualisation literacy β=0.322, t=2.946, p=.004; **self-reported ChatGPT-literacy β=−0.159, t=−1.579,
  p=.118 (n.s., and negative)**; baseline no-AI score β=0.098, p=.367 (n.s.).
  Two things follow. (i) A *knowledge* test predicted AI-assisted task performance and a *self-report
  scale* did not — strong support for Foray being a performance exam at all. (ii) GLAT scores nothing
  about the interaction process; it is answered before the task.
- **AICOS — "Objective Measurement of AI Literacy"**, arXiv:2503.12921v2, *Computers and Education: AI*
  2025, doi:10.1016/j.caeai.2025.100485. **UNVERIFIED (abstract only).** Explicitly built because
  existing tools have "limitations regarding subjective data collection methods." Again an objective
  *knowledge/competency* test, not a process measure.
- **MAILS / Ng et al. / ChatGPT Literacy Scale**: self-report by construction. The Lee & Park ChatGPT
  Literacy Scale is the one GLAT beat head-to-head (see above). I did not fetch MAILS itself
  (ScienceDirect blocked both direct fetch attempts); do **not** cite MAILS numbers from this document.

**Bottom line on E:** the field's move away from self-report went to *tests taken before the task*, not
to *scores computed from the task log*. Foray's `processSignal` is in a category the literature has
identified as desirable (Chen & Jia: "current assessments largely rely on self-reported scales, lacking
insights into how literacy manifests in actual learning processes") and has not yet delivered.

## F. What I looked for and did NOT find — reportable negatives

- **No study validates a count-based process score against an artefact outcome under stakes.** Every
  positive prompt-count result comes from coursework or a contest, where inflating the count carries a
  time cost and no reward. The exam case is untested.
- **No inter-rater or test-retest reliability figures exist for any log-derived process score used as a
  graded component.** The nearest numbers are Fleiss κ=0.29 (TOJET prompt rubric), Cohen's κ=0.75
  (Chen & Jia's prompt-act coding, research coding not grading), and "acceptable" unquantified
  inter-rater agreement (Li et al. 2025).
- **No published work computes anything resembling Foray's exact formula**
  `0.5·min(1, prompts/N) + 0.5·min(1, cycles/M)`, nor any capped/saturating process score, and then
  reports its correlation with a score of record. Searched OpenAlex, arXiv, Crossref, Semantic Scholar
  (rate-limited), and Exa.
- **No study found tests whether candidates inflate a process metric when told it is scored.** This is
  the gaming/construct-irrelevant-variance question and it appears to be genuinely open. The closest
  adjacent literature is "gaming the system" in ITS research, which I did not search and which is about
  hint abuse, not prompt volume.
- **The hiring-manager "inverted signal" concern has no direct published counterpart** in what I
  searched. Fan et al.'s metacognitive laziness and Yang et al.'s negative originality–DoK correlation
  are the nearest published analogues, and neither is about hiring.

## G. Recommendation implied by the evidence (one paragraph, clearly labelled as inference)

The literature will support "we score the process from the interaction log" (Chen & Jia, Li et al.,
Fan et al. all argue process data is where competence shows) and will support "we use a performance
task rather than a self-report scale" (GLAT, AICOS). It will **not** support "and volume is the right
functional form." The two studies that back prompt count both show it confounded with prompt quality
(r≈0.74–0.75 in Yang; r=0.50 with context in Choi et al.), the one study with an expert-rated outcome
found r≈−0.01, and the one study that actually re-rated artefacts after a revision cycle found the
cycle helped a third of the time. If the 25 points must stay model-free, the defensible published
precedent is **structural, not count-based**: Chen & Jia's discriminating signal was the co-occurrence
of refinement commands with clarification questions, and Jiang et al.'s was balance/pacing rather than
turn count. Both are computable from a log without a model call. Neither has criterion validity yet —
which means Foray would be making a claim no one has published, and should say so.
