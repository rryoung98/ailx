# Q2 — Does interaction VOLUME correlate with QUALITY?

**Scope:** evidence for/against scoring Foray T1's `processSignal` as a monotone function of
`distinctPrompts` and `cycles`.

---

## VERDICT

**Volume is not a defensible proxy for quality. Across all four literatures the raw COUNT of
interactions is a null-to-negative predictor of outcome quality, while normalised RATES and
behaviour KINDS carry the signal.** The single most direct measurement available (Ziegler et
al. 2022, n = 1,780–2,047 developers) shows raw suggestion volume `shown` at **r = 0.01,
p = 0.75** and time-on-task `active_hour` at **r = −0.05, p = 0.03** against perceived
productivity, while the RATIO `accepted_per_shown` reaches **ρ = 0.24, p < 0.0001**. Same data,
same population: the ratio predicts and the count does not.

Direction by literature:

| Literature | Volume → quality |
|---|---|
| 1. Human–LLM chat | **Null / flat.** Longer conversations do not raise quality; satisfaction saturates. |
| 2. Programming with LLMs | **Null to negative.** Raw counts ~0; time-on-task negative; the two RCT outcomes are *less* time and *fewer* defects. |
| 3. Intelligent tutoring systems (deepest well) | **Strongly negative.** More actions/hints/practice is the canonical signature of *worse* learning. r up to −0.61. |
| 4. Writing-process / keystroke logs | **Mixed, mostly weak.** Time-on-task r ≈ 0.40–0.52 in some corpora, but a direct replication found ALL revision/volume features near-null (max r = 0.24, no model beat baseline). |

**Consequence for T1.** `0.5·min(1, distinctPrompts/N) + 0.5·min(1, cycles/M)` is the exact
functional form that the ITS literature spent twenty years learning to detect and penalise:
it pays for practice opportunities without checking whether any of them moved the student
toward mastery. A candidate who fires N distinct prompts and M revisions with no improvement
scores identically to one who did the same and shipped. Beck & Gong's wheel-spinning
construct and Baker's gaming construct are both defined by high action counts with no
progress; both are negatively associated with learning. Nothing found in this search
supports a volume-monotone process signal. Two findings (Perry et al.; Ziegler et al.)
suggest what a defensible replacement measures: **the kind of change between consecutive
prompts, and counts normalised by an offer/opportunity denominator** — not totals.

## 1. Human–AI interaction: does turn count predict success?

### 1.1 Huang, Lin, He, Huang & Huang (2024) — SUPPORTS-NOT / null
- *How Does Conversation Length Impact User's Satisfaction? A Case Study of Length-Controlled
  Conversations with LLM-Powered Chatbots.* CHI '24 Extended Abstracts.
  doi:10.1145/3613905.3650823
- **UNVERIFIED (abstract + publisher highlights only; ACM DL full text CAPTCHA-blocked).**
- Design: two GPT-4 Slack chatbots, conversation length experimentally fixed at **0, 3, 5 and
  7 turns**. Users asked highly-conversable and less-conversable questions.
- Exact finding: "conversation quality does not differ drastically across different
  conditions"; "conversation length does not significantly enhance quality for high-
  conversability questions"; "self-reported satisfaction increased with longer conversations
  only up to a certain point"; "the ideal number of follow-up questions (N) is
  context-dependent and not fixed."
- **CONTRADICTS** a fixed cap `N`. This is an experimental manipulation of exactly the variable
  T1 scores, and it moved quality by roughly nothing. It also directly refutes the assumption
  that a single N or M can be chosen for all tasks.

### 1.2 Laban, Hayashi, Zhou & Neville (2025) — CONTRADICTS
- *LLMs Get Lost In Multi-Turn Conversation.* arXiv:2505.06120.
- **UNVERIFIED (abstract only; full PDF not fetched).**
- Exact finding: across six generation tasks and **200,000+ simulated conversations**, every
  top open- and closed-weight model shows "an average drop of **39%**" in multi-turn versus
  single-turn performance, decomposed into "a minor loss in aptitude and a significant
  increase in unreliability." Models "make assumptions in early turns and prematurely attempt
  to generate final solutions, on which they overly rely."
- **CONTRADICTS.** This is a machine-side result, not a human-skill result, but it matters for
  T1: extra turns degrade the artefact through the model, so a candidate who takes more turns
  may be fighting a degrading context, not demonstrating craft. Rewarding turn count rewards
  entering the regime where the assistant is measurably worse.

### 1.3 *A word to the wise* (2025), *System* — QUALIFIES  
*(author names not verified — publisher page returned no author metadata)*
- *A word to the wise: Crafting impactful prompts for ChatGPT.* System (Elsevier),
  https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0346251X25001666
- **UNVERIFIED (abstract + ScienceDirect preview only; paywalled).**
- 238 prompts from 10 Korean EFL graduate students; one-way ANOVA, correlation, network
  analysis, K-means. Learners used **2–8 prompts per session, mean 3.5**.
- Exact finding: high-satisfaction prompts used "clear, interconnected strategies"
  (contextualisation; specifying style, tone, length). "**Repetition correlated with
  dissatisfaction.**"
- **CONTRADICTS** volume specifically. Repeating yourself — which raises a raw prompt count and
  survives naive dedup only weakly — tracked *worse* outcomes.

## 2. Programming with LLMs

### 2.1 Ziegler, Kalliamvakou, Simister, Sittampalam, Li, Rice, Rifkin & Aftandilian (2022) — CONTRADICTS. **The strongest single citation.**
- *Productivity Assessment of Neural Code Completion.* MAPS '22 (co-located PLDI).
  arXiv:2205.06537. Full text fetched.
- n = 2,631 survey responses from GitHub Copilot users matched to IDE telemetry; correlation
  analyses run on n = 1,763–2,047 depending on metric. Outcome = aggregate self-reported
  productivity. Copilot acceptance rate 27%, mean DCPU > 31.
- Headline: "acceptance rate of shown suggestions is a better predictor of perceived
  productivity than the alternative measures"; `accepted_per_shown` **ρ = 0.24, P < 0.0001**,
  best of all metrics.
- **The Appendix B table is the finding for us.** Ranked, from the fetched table:

  | metric | kind | n | r | p |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | `accepted_per_shown` | **rate** | 1,780 | **0.24** | <0.0001 |
  | `accepted_per_opportunity` | rate | 1,789 | 0.22 | <0.0001 |
  | `accepted_char_per_active_hour` | rate | 1,789 | 0.16 | <0.0001 |
  | `accepted` | **raw count** | 1,789 | **0.11** | <0.0001 |
  | `accepted_char` | raw count | 1,789 | 0.11 | <0.0001 |
  | `shown` | **raw count** | 1,789 | **0.01** | **0.75 (n.s.)** |
  | `opportunity` | raw count | 1,789 | −0.04 | 0.08 |
  | `active_hour` | **time on task** | 1,789 | **−0.05** | **0.03 (sig. negative)** |

- Exact reading: within one dataset, **the ratio explains ~4× the variance of the corresponding
  raw count (0.24² vs 0.11²), the raw volume of AI interactions is statistically
  indistinguishable from zero, and time spent is significantly NEGATIVE.** The authors also
  note "persistence over shorter time periods is generally better than over longer periods…
  shorter periods move the measure closer to acceptance rate."
- Caveat to state honestly: the outcome is **perceived** productivity (SPACE survey), not an
  independently graded artefact, and correlations are modest in absolute terms.
- **CONTRADICTS** volume-monotone scoring as directly as any published number I found.

### 2.2 Becker, Rush, Barnes & Rein (METR, 2025) — CONTRADICTS
- *Measuring the Impact of Early-2025 AI on Experienced Open-Source Developer Productivity.*
  arXiv:2507.09089v2. Full text fetched.
- RCT: 16 experienced developers, **246 tasks** on mature repos (avg 23,000 stars, 5 years'
  prior experience), each task randomised to AI-allowed (Cursor Pro + Claude 3.5/3.7) or not.
  136 issues completed AI-allowed, 110 AI-disallowed.
- Exact finding: developers forecast a **24%** speed-up, estimated **20%** after the fact, and
  were in fact **19% SLOWER** with AI. Experts forecast 38–39% speed-up.
- From 74 valid screen recordings (84 hours, ~10-second label resolution): with AI allowed,
  developers "spend a smaller proportion of their time actively coding and reading/searching
  for information. Instead, they spend time **reviewing AI outputs, prompting AI systems, and
  waiting for AI generations**", plus more idle time.
- **CONTRADICTS.** The recorded increase in prompting activity coincided with a 19% worse
  outcome. A T1 process signal computed from that same recording would have scored the slower,
  AI-allowed condition HIGHER. The paper also documents a ~39-point gap between perceived and
  actual benefit — the exact failure mode described by the hiring managers.

### 2.3 Peng, Kalliamvakou, Cihon & Demirer (2023) — QUALIFIES (and inverts the metric)
- *The Impact of AI on Developer Productivity: Evidence from GitHub Copilot.* arXiv:2302.06590.
  Full text fetched.
- RCT: recruited developers implement an HTTP server in JavaScript "as quickly as possible."
  Correctness = a fixed suite of **12 tests**; completion time = repo creation → first commit
  passing all 12.
- Exact finding: treatment (Copilot) group **55.8% faster**, 95% CI **21–89%**. Largest gains
  for less experienced, older, and heavier-coding developers.
- **QUALIFIES.** AI access helps — but note what the study treats as good: **less time**. The
  measured quality bar is binary (12/12 tests) and does not vary with interaction volume.
  Under T1's formula the *slow* control group would earn no process credit and the fast
  Copilot group's score would depend on how many prompts they happened to fire, which the
  paper never links to the outcome.

### 2.4 Vaithilingam, Zhang & Glassman (2022) — CONTRADICTS
- *Expectation vs. Experience: Evaluating the Usability of Code Generation Tools Powered by
  Large Language Models.* CHI '22 Extended Abstracts. Full text fetched.
- Within-subjects study, **24 participants**, three task difficulties, 20-minute cap.
- Exact finding: "Copilot did not necessarily improve the task completion time or success
  rate." Failures: Copilot **5** vs Intellisense **2** (n.s.). Copilot task breakdown:
  8/8 easy, 6/8 medium, 5/8 hard completed; Intellisense 8/8, 8/8, 6/8. **3 of the 5 Copilot
  failures were caused by incorrect generated code that "led participants into a
  time-consuming debugging rabbit hole."** Completed tasks were faster on average.
- **CONTRADICTS.** The debugging rabbit hole is a high-interaction, low-quality state. It
  generates many prompts and many revise cycles and ends in task failure.

### 2.5 Perry, Srivastava, Kumar & Boneh (2023) — QUALIFIES, and points at the fix
- *Do Users Write More Insecure Code with AI Assistants?* CCS '23. arXiv:2211.03622v3. Full
  text fetched.
- Large-scale user study, five security tasks across languages, codex-davinci-002.
- Exact findings: participants with AI access "wrote significantly less secure code" and "were
  more likely to believe they wrote secure code." Participants "who trusted the AI less and
  engaged more with the language and format of their prompts (e.g. re-phrasing, adjusting
  temperature) provided code with fewer security vulnerabilities." Prompt-repair taxonomy:
  *expand scope / reduce scope / reword / change type*; "changes in type were the most common
  repair strategy."
- **Important negative result:** Table 5D reports mean number of prompts per user by prompt
  category and by question, and the authors interpret it only as showing "**variance across
  questions**… the specific security task influences how users choose to format their prompts."
  **They report no relationship between prompt COUNT and security.** The predictive variables
  are prompt *type*, *scope change*, temperature adjustment and edit distance from model
  output.
- **QUALIFIES.** This is the closest published thing to a validated AI-process signal, and it
  is explicitly about the KIND of successive prompt edit, not the number of them. It also
  shows prompt count is task-dependent, so a fixed N is mis-calibrated across tasks by
  construction.

### 2.6 GitClear (2025) — QUALIFIES, weak evidence grade
- *AI Copilot Code Quality: 2025 Look Back at 12 Months of Data.*
  https://www.gitclear.com/ai_assistant_code_quality_2025_research
- **PARTIAL FETCH — the public page is a gated teaser; the numeric body requires an email.
  Only what is on the public page is reported here.** Industry report, not peer reviewed, no
  stated causal identification.
- Public claims: **211 million changed lines**, 2020–2024, from repos at Google, Microsoft,
  Meta and enterprise C-Corps. "**4x more code cloning**"; "'copy/paste' exceeds 'moved' code
  for the first time in history"; "a spike in the prevalence of duplicate code blocks, along
  with increases in short-term churn code, and the continued decline of moved lines."
- **QUALIFIES with a caveat.** Suggestive that higher AI throughput coincides with worse
  structural quality, but this is a correlational industry report over a period when many
  things changed. **Do not lean on it.** I would not cite it as load-bearing.

## 3. Intelligent tutoring systems — the deepest and most damning well

### 3.1 Baker, Corbett & Koedinger (2004) — CONTRADICTS
- *Detecting Student Misuse of Intelligent Tutoring Systems.* ITS 2004 / Intelligent Tutoring
  Systems LNCS. https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~rsbaker/BCK2004MLFinal.pdf — full text fetched.
- Defines **"gaming the system"**: "behavior aimed at obtaining correct answers and advancing
  within the tutoring curriculum by systematically taking advantage of regularities in the
  software's feedback and help."
- Exact finding: "Students who frequently engage in behavior termed 'gaming the system'…
  **learn only 2/3 as much** as similar students who do not engage in such behaviors" —
  "controlling for prior knowledge and general academic ability."
- Also, from classroom observation: "a student's frequency of gaming was **strongly negatively
  correlated with learning**, but was not correlated with the frequency of other off-task
  behavior; nor was other off-task behavior significantly correlated with learning."
- Describes **help abuse**: "a student quickly and repeatedly asks for help until the tutor
  gives the student the correct answer, often before the student attempts the problem on his
  or her own."
- **CONTRADICTS, and this is the design lesson.** Gaming is *defined* as maximising a
  volume-monotone system metric without doing the work. A `processSignal` that pays per
  deduped prompt and per revise cycle is a gameable metric of exactly this shape, and the
  cheapest strategy against it is help abuse.

### 3.2 Aleven, McLaren, Roll & Koedinger (2004) — CONTRADICTS, best correlation numbers
- *Applying Cognitive Modeling to Meta-Cognitive Skills.* ITS 2004.
  http://cs.cmu.edu/~bmclaren/pubs/AlevenEtAl-HelpSeeking-ITS2004.pdf — full text fetched.
- Data: Geometry Cognitive Tutor, ~7 hours per student, **49 students (40 with both pre- and
  post-test)**, ~**47,500 logged actions**. Learning gain LG = (Post − Pre)/(1 − Pre),
  mean 0.41, SD 0.28. A 57-production-rule help-seeking model classifies each action.
- Exact findings:
  - **72%** of all student actions were unproductive help-seeking ("buggy") behaviour.
  - Breakdown: **Help Abuse 37%** (of which "clicking through" hints = 33%), Try-Step Abuse
    18%, Help Avoidance 11%, misc. 6%.
  - **Correlations with learning gain: Total Meta-cognitive Bugs r = −0.61; Help Abuse
    r = −0.46; Misc. Bugs r = −0.47 (both p < 0.01); Try-Step Abuse r = 0.02 (n.s.);
    Help Avoidance r = −0.10 (n.s.).**
  - Cognitive-level success correlated with learning at **r = 0.58, p = 0.0001** — "about the
    same" as help-seeking behaviour. The two were correlated **r = 0.78, p < 0.0001**, and a
    multiple regression combining them "accounted only for marginally more variance than
    either one alone."
- **CONTRADICTS.** The dominant unproductive behaviour is *clicking through hints* — pure
  interaction volume with no cognition. Its correlation with learning is −0.46. Note also that
  the *opposite* extreme, Help Avoidance, is **not** significant (r = −0.10): the relationship
  is not "less volume is better" either. **Volume in both directions is a poor predictor; the
  productive/unproductive distinction is what carries the −0.61.**

### 3.3 Beck & Gong (2013), wheel-spinning — CONTRADICTS
- *Wheel-Spinning: Students Who Fail to Master a Skill.* AIED 2013, LNCS 7926, pp. 431–440.
  doi:10.1007/978-3-642-39112-5_44. Verified to exist via DOI and researchr; **156–163
  citations**.
- **The original PDF could not be fetched (Springer / ResearchGate blocked). The numbers below
  are quoted from two OTHER papers whose full text I DID fetch — label them SECONDARY.**
- Definition: wheel-spinning = a student "has spent a considerable amount of time practicing a
  skill, yet displays little or no progress towards mastery"; operationally, ≥10 practice
  opportunities on a skill without mastering it.
- Secondary quote (Kai et al., §3.4 below): "Beck & Gong (2013)… found that **approximately 38%
  of students did not master a given skill after 10 practice opportunities** and were thus
  potentially wheel-spinning."
- Gong & Beck (2015), *Towards Detecting Wheel-Spinning*, LAK '15, doi:10.1145/2724660.2724673
  (abstract fetched): wheel-spinning "has been shown to be a common problem affecting a
  significant number of students in different tutoring systems and is **negatively associated
  with learning**."
- **CONTRADICTS.** Wheel-spinning is the ITS name for the failure mode T1's formula rewards:
  many practice opportunities, zero progress. It is common (38%), detectable, and bad.

### 3.4 Kai, Almeda, Baker, C. Heffernan & N. Heffernan (2018) — CONTRADICTS
- *Decision Tree Modeling of Wheel-Spinning and Productive Persistence in Skill Builders.*
  Journal of Educational Data Mining. Full text fetched via
  https://learninganalytics.upenn.edu/ryanbaker/210-Article%20Text-1526-1-18-20180624_V7.pdf
- Data: ASSISTments, **287,093 student–problem-set pairs**, 75,481 with ARRS information.
- Exact findings:
  - Under Beck & Gong's count-based definition (>10 problems without 3 correct in a row),
    **46.9%** of the 75,481 ARRS pairs — and **12.3%** of all 287,093 pairs — would be labelled
    wheel-spinning. Under the authors' refined definition only **9.1%** are. The authors say
    plainly that the count-based rule over-flags, because "many of these students do indeed
    eventually get three correct in a row."
  - Decision tree: "**Students with more bottom-out hints requested in the last 8 problems are
    more likely to wheel-spin.** About **89.74%** of instances (2,781 of 3,099
    student–problem-set pairs) were included in this branch." A separate branch —
    no hints requested in at least one problem, never two or more bottom-out hints — labelled
    **76.49%** (5,958 of 7,789) as wheel-spinning.
- **CONTRADICTS, twice over.** (a) More help requests predict the unproductive state. (b) A
  purely COUNT-based threshold mislabels productive persistence as wheel-spinning at 5× the
  rate of a behaviour-aware definition (46.9% vs 9.1%). That is a direct measurement of how
  badly a count-only rule performs against a behaviour-aware one on the same data.

### 3.5 Wang, Kai & Baker (2020) — QUALIFIES
- *Early Detection of Wheel-Spinning in ASSISTments.* AIED 2020, LNCS.
  doi:10.1007/978-3-030-52237-7_46. Full text fetched via PMC7334145.
- Exact findings: a detector built from the **first nine practice opportunities** outperforms
  models using less data, but wheel-spinners can be separated from productive persisters from
  only the **first three** opportunities; wheel-spinning vs non-persistence from **five**;
  non-persistence vs productive persistence from **seven**. "These detectors relied upon hint
  requests, the correctness of prior opportunities, and the amount of practice and time on the
  skill."
- **QUALIFIES.** Volume features (amount of practice, time on skill) do appear — but only
  *jointly with correctness*, i.e. as inputs to a productive/unproductive classifier, never as
  a monotone reward. This is the shape a defensible T1 signal would have to take.

## 4. Writing-process research: keystroke and revision logs

### 4.1 Conijn, Cook, van Zaanen & Van Waes (2022) — CONTRADICTS. Direct null result.
- *Early prediction of writing quality using keystroke logging.* International Journal of
  Artificial Intelligence in Education. doi:10.1007/s40593-021-00268-w. Full text fetched.
  (Van Waes is a co-author of Inputlog.)
- Design: **126** participants (from 141 recruited), academic-writing course, timed academic
  summarisation task, **54 keystroke features** drawn from prior literature — including
  revision counts, backspaces, deletions, production cycles, pauses.
- Exact findings:
  - Over the **full** writing process, **only one** of 54 features correlated significantly
    with final grade: percentage of long pauses between words, **r = 0.22**. Everything else,
    including all revision-volume features, was non-significant.
  - Split by phase, the largest correlations found anywhere were **|r| ≤ 0.24** (SD of IKI
    r = 0.24; number of production cycles r = 0.18; percentage of linear transitions between
    sentences r = −0.18 and −0.24; SD of time between sentences r = −0.20).
  - **"None of the models outperformed the baseline (mean final grade) at any timepoint"** for
    regression. Classification "only slightly better than the majority class baseline
    (**highest AUC = 0.57**)".
  - Authors' conclusion: "the relationship between keystroke data and writing quality might be
    **less clear than previously posited**."
- **CONTRADICTS.** This is the closest available analogue to T1: an automated, model-free
  process signal built from behaviour logs, validated against a human grade. It does not work.
  AUC 0.57 against a 52% majority baseline is near-chance.

### 4.2 The prior literature it contradicts — QUALIFIES (secondary, via 4.1)
- Guo, Deane, van Rijn, Zhang & Bennett (2018), *Journal of Educational Measurement* 55(2),
  194–216; Sinharay, Zhang & Deane (2019), *Applied Measurement in Education* 32(2), 116–137.
- **UNVERIFIED — quoted from Conijn et al.'s fetched text, not fetched directly.**
- Exact quoted finding: "total time on task has been shown to be positively correlated with
  writing scores in several studies, with correlations ranging from **0.40 to 0.52**"; also
  burst length **r = 0.38**.
- Also quoted: Baaijen & Galbraith (2018) extracted sentence-production and global-linearity
  factors that "did not show significant correlations with text quality."
- **QUALIFIES — this is the strongest pro-volume evidence found anywhere in this search, and
  it is (a) about time-on-task, not action counts, (b) in *timed standardised essay* settings
  where time-on-task mostly proxies effort and word count, and (c) failed to replicate in 4.1.**
  Note the sign conflict with Ziegler's `active_hour` r = −0.05: time-on-task helps when the
  task is unaided writing and hurts when an AI assistant is in the loop.

## What was searched and NOT found

- **No study found that validates a monotone count-based AI-interaction process score against
  an independently rated artefact.** I searched HCI, SE, EDM/AIED and writing-process
  literatures. If it exists I did not surface it, and its absence is itself the finding: T1's
  `processSignal` has no published validation precedent.
- **No published study found that measures the specific hiring-manager inversion claim**
  ("high-token-spend vibecoders score well, careful engineers are penalised") as a scored
  outcome. METR §2.2 and Perry §2.5 are the nearest evidence and both are indirect.
- Beck & Gong (2013) original PDF, Zamfirescu-Pereira et al. "Why Johnny Can't Prompt"
  (CHI 2023) full text, and the GitClear numeric body were all blocked. Claims that depend on
  them are labelled UNVERIFIED or SECONDARY above, or omitted.

## Recommended framing for the team

The honest one-liner: *"Every literature we checked that measured this found interaction
volume to be a null-to-negative predictor of quality; the ITS field has a twenty-year-old name
for high-volume/no-progress behaviour and it is used to detect FAILURE, not merit."*

If T1 must keep a model-free process signal, the two fetched sources that show what works
point the same way:
1. **Normalise by an opportunity denominator** (Ziegler: rate ρ = 0.24 vs count r = 0.01).
2. **Classify the KIND of successive prompt edit** — expand scope / reduce scope / reword /
   change type (Perry) — and pair any volume feature with a correctness or progress feature
   (Wang/Kai/Baker), never reward it alone.
